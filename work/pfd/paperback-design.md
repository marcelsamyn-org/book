# Paperback wrap design constraints

The reader must recognize the book on a shelf, then understand its question when they turn it over. The approved front cover is the visual reference. The back copy is a proposal for author review.

- R1, cognitive load: give the back one hook, three short paragraphs, and one closing thought. Keep artwork below the copy. **Citations:** Sweller (1988) for extraneous load; Cowan (2010) for working-memory limits.
- R2, first impression: preserve the front's tactile indigo, cream type, and sparse gold. Keep the spine identifiable at shelf size. **Citations:** Lindgaard et al. (2006) for rapid visual judgments.
- R3, fluency: reuse Lora and IBM Plex, with generous text margins and a quiet background. **Citations:** Alter and Oppenheimer (2009) for processing fluency; Spence (2011) for sensory coherence.
- R4, expectations: signal a reflective nonfiction book. Use the manuscript's actual questions and personal experience, without invented endorsements or credentials. **Citations:** Clark (2013) for predictive processing; Seckler et al. (2015) for visual coherence and trust.
- R5, decision: make the question clear, then show the scope: learning, work, love, creativity. Preserve the uncertainty explored by the book. **Citations:** Trope and Liberman (2010) for concrete language near decisions.

The design uses a separate quiet back texture and a plain indigo spine. Type stays editable in Typst. A white barcode reserve makes the unfinished production detail visible without inventing a barcode.

These are design hypotheses, not measured reader outcomes.
