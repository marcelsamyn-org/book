# Introduction
I thought it was pretty normal to have a headache a few times per week and blamed it on imperfect sleep. One day it was so painful I threw up and went to the ER, where they dismissed it as “stress-related” and gave me painkillers. That removed the daytime pain but I’d still wake up every night, my head spinning with the thought of “is it possible that a skull can just…crack?” When I lost some of my balance and couldn’t walk straight anymore, they finally agreed to do an MRI scan of my brain.

“We found something. Can you stay in the hospital?”

Turns out there was a tumor that needed to be removed. Quickly. My neurosurgeon moved two months’ worth of appointments so I could be treated the next weekday.

Apart from that, the medical team kept me quite unaware of the gravity of the whole situation. I felt relieved more than anything—finally, we found why I have headaches—and the surgeon gave me a lot of confidence that it could be removed without major long-term consequences. I remember scrolling through the MRI images trying to find the “speck” they found, to no avail. The day before the surgery, the surgeon had to interrupt me at some point to say “no Marcel, this is actually a big one,” gesturing with his hands. It turned out I couldn’t find the speck because it was so large I mistook it for a regular part of the brain. Then, only a week or so after surgery, I read an article in the newspaper describing how my neurosurgeon had to fight with other surgeons for space in intensive care for me because “if we don’t operate now, there’s this young 25-year-old man who can end up in a coma.”

That was the moment the real gravity of the situation hit me, and if I’d never known this, the meaning I created from this chapter of my life would have been entirely different.

There’s something special about crises like this. Near-death, heartbreak, catastrophic business failure—those challenges we couldn’t possibly predict and that make us question everything. Suddenly we’re confronted with our own imperfection, we notice the sure path isn’t so sure after all or we discover that we’ll never be able to get or do what we’ve always wanted. If we’re lucky enough to come out of these crises with a smile on our face, we emerge with something deeply valuable on our hands: a visceral knowledge that life is short, patience for ourselves and those around us, the resolution to give our love despite everything trying to sabotage it.

As you can imagine, that episode in my life was deeply meaningful. I received an abundance of love from the people dearest to me, I understood both the fragility of life and the power of the human body, I learned to be patient with myself when healing and I felt the worst pains I’ve ever felt in my life—and got through it. To this day, when people see me walk in the cold in a T-shirt and ask “aren’t you cold?” I just reply cheekily with “I’ve had worse pain in my life.” Would I wish something like this happened to anyone else? Would I *want* to experience it again? Absolutely not. It doesn’t make any sense to want something like this another time…but still, there’s *something* to it. When we say something is “sacred,” we mean that it’s significant in a way that stands outside of rationality or reason. You should not treat something sacred as something to optimize, or something to fix. It often doesn’t make sense, you can’t argue with it and yet, somehow it *has* to exist.

This book builds up to its title, but it starts with a simpler question: what makes something meaningful?

As humans, we mold the world to our liking. We solve problems, we prevent pain, we always keep on finding ways to improve our comfort—and we find deep meaning in doing this. Artificial Intelligence is the latest breakthrough in that process and it looks like it’s the first one that actually has a shot at delivering: maybe…we soon won’t face any struggle at all.

That’s a pipe dream but even if we never get to solve *all* the problems in the world, AI will likely help us solve a significant portion of them. So instead of arguing about how good AI will become, let’s assume it will become perfect. Let’s assume all our problems will be solved and AI will become infinitely capable.

Because when we go there, we’ll discover core things we desperately need to understand about our own life satisfaction and what it means to live a meaningful life. Even if AI ends up not getting any better or cheaper than today, that’s still something worth knowing.

This book started as an explanation of the limitations of AI (specifically, large language models) that would stand the test of time as the technology keeps improving at breakneck speed. That core is still there—building up from the technology’s base structure—but it has turned into a discovery of what it actually is that gives us the deepest sense of life satisfaction. Assuming a perfect technology exists has forced me to drive straight past what even the biggest thinkers in the personal growth space teach, and ended up giving me some profound insights that I rarely see discussed—especially not with the clarity and structure that a technology-first angle provides.

I hope you’ll find it as fascinating as I did writing it.

*How* I’m writing this book is a bit of a weird situation. Language models today are excellent writers, but I quickly discovered that having AI write this would counter all of the points I’m making in this book. So these words are typed by hand, and only because I made that decision early on has the book been able to evolve and become what it is now. Only because it’s so difficult to come up with what to write next—while knowing an infinitely fast and extremely intelligent writing machine is just a single click away—has this process been as fulfilling to me as it is.

I still use plenty of AI, even for the book. In fact I’ve built a whole system around this to help me track my progress and estimate how much I need to write on any given day. The initial release date for the book was decided by a calculation Claude made. It probably came up with the title, I don’t even remember anymore. The research is done and collected by AI—I just double-check, reword and make sure it makes sense in the flow of the book. And I’m definitely feeling token anxiety (tokens are the ‘currency’ that AI usage is counted in—you’ll read about that): Claude and ChatGPT are there in the background, idling while my monthly token allowance is not being used as I’m pressing key by key for each letter I want the book to have.

Between when I started writing and when I finished this book, large language models improved tremendously and my day job completely changed. I stopped using AI for small file edits here and there, or delegating precise coding tasks, and I started giving it entire vision documents and dozens of tasks to work on at the same time. I started to trust its ability to read through the entire book, not only finding grammatical mistakes but also giving useful feedback on the story flow and style of the book.

Simultaneously I’m noticing in myself the mental consequences of continuously working with highly intelligent AI, eerily close to what I was expecting to happen. I’m losing the willingness to sit still and think—I just want to delegate all thinking—while actually I need to think *more deeply*—the simple things are easy to delegate, the difficult things remain for me. It’s a rather sobering situation to realize that I have to forcibly slow myself down and be less productive (even though producing feels satisfying) for the sake of my happiness. The thing that society and work push me to maximize became ridiculously easy to fulfill, but I cannot let myself…for my own sake. Funny how I need to exercise self-control so I can be less productive.

Anyways, we’ll dive into all that.

You’ll learn to understand AI—even if you’re not a computer scientist—by seeing how its “brain” works, so you deeply get why it acts certain ways (even for future versions). We’ll share plenty of fun examples, you’ll understand the core of human happiness, and when all of that comes together, I think you’ll see that we end up at some pretty wild stuff.

This book is *not* for you if you just want to understand how to be more productive at work with AI (though you will implicitly learn that), nor if you’re purely looking for a philosophical approach that ignores the subtle effects of our current AI (though if you want, you can skip Part 1 and even Part 2). If you hate jokes, best to walk away too. Note that I try to be accurate and complete when I’m making statements, but often I like to slightly exaggerate things. That’s not meant to state “this is how it is,” it’s meant to evoke a *feeling* so you understand the *direction* of the argument, not the extent.

That said, strap in. We’re going for a ride.

## Concepts and Terminology

This world of “AI” is wrapped in hype, mystery and confusion. From charlatans slapping the word on every product they find to the researchers not even knowing why these systems end up seeming so smart, you’ll find every opinion and perspective under the sun in a matter of seconds.

So I’ll do what every sensible person would do: add one more perspective. I’ll gradually build up the nomenclature from the broad terms to precise technical ones. Then you’ll be able to understand the odd behavior of our AI systems, and all that together will help us find an answer for the big question: how we can find happiness in a world driven by AI.

Let’s start with this word, *Artificial Intelligence*. When I took the course with that name in university I was expecting to learn how magic works. We didn’t have ChatGPT yet but the groundwork for it existed: we already had some seemingly magical technologies for things like translation and image recognition. Alas, this course turned out to be more about things like optimizing the logic for finding the best chess moves. However, I did learn a definition of AI that turned out to be the most useful one in today’s day and age: AI is where a system *appears to be behaving in an intelligent way*. This definition is wildly ambiguous, perfectly reflecting this word. It doesn’t say anything about how we know if something is intelligent or what kind of technology it should be. Maybe you say that the lights turning on automatically as you walk into a room is intelligent—and I can’t disagree with that.

So what is “intelligence,” you say? Finding a definition for intelligence has proven to be just as difficult as building Artificial Intelligence itself. Every time someone comes up with a test (a benchmark, as we’ll see later) that is *so difficult* it would finally tell us when something or someone is intelligent, it ends up being aced by AI just a few months later. Yet, we let humans interact with the AI through chatbots and they quickly feel their stupidity. So in this book, interpret words like “intelligent” and “smart” vaguely—because it’s the best we can do in this context. I use them more in the sense of “it feels to humans like it’s intelligent.” When I talk about how AI is *becoming* more intelligent, I mean that it’s getting better and better at all these tests—that’s a clear indication of improvement.

Now that we’ve satisfied the salespeople and saddened the scientists, let’s turn to some more precise terms. *Machine Learning* is an approach to programming where you’re not telling the computer what to do explicitly, but you’re showing it examples and you let it figure out how to do it by itself. Most machine learning is “supervised” where we give it the correct input and output (when a person asks this, you should say that) and it can also be unsupervised when we don’t even know the correct answer.

Most of the magical and weird AI things we see today have come from a third type: *reinforcement learning*. In reinforcement learning (RL), you usually let the machine do its thing for a bit and then you give it a score for how well it’s doing. This is, for example, how robots learn to walk: you give the machine control over the motors and you score it, say, on how well it’s still standing upright and if it’s actually moving forwards. Reinforcement learning gives the AI a lot of freedom to “just figure it out” and this has been how the AI systems that find new cheats at video games or break out of their containment computers have been built.

So in normal software, we write *code* that says exactly how the machine should behave. It’s a step-by-step plan for a computer to follow. In machine learning instead, we *train models*. A model is just a file with a whole bunch of numbers—there’s still code, of course, but the code is relatively simple: just multiply and add those numbers in a certain pre-defined order. It’s a mathematical property that, with enough numbers like this (we call them *parameters* or *weights*), you can compute anything, so the training step comes down to calculating what values these parameters should have. Depending on the training data you give the model, it will learn to perform different tasks.

A machine learning model is trained through *error minimization*. We give it an example input, calculate the output, then we check how wrong the output is, and then we make a small tweak to the parameters so that the output would not be (as) wrong. That’s training: you take a couple trillion examples, and you keep adjusting the model so that its error would be as small as possible. The hope is that (and it ends up being true) when we minimize the error on enough training examples, the model has learned a general way of doing what it needs to do and it can do the right thing when you give it inputs that are not part of the examples.

Once this model is done training, these parameters don’t change anymore. They are frozen. There is no inherent “continuous learning as it runs.” To do this you have to re-train or do some other trickery.

You probably also encountered the terms *neural network* or *deep learning*—or even *deep neural networks.* These are roughly the same thing: a neural network is a specific structure of a machine learning algorithm (the algorithm, here, is the *code* part of the model—it defines *how* the parameters should be added together), and a *deep* neural network is just a bigger version of that. There are many kinds of machine learning algorithms but neural networks are very general and can be used for pretty much anything, though the trade-off is that you need a lot more training for it to actually learn to do something useful.

All of the “headline systems” you see today are deep neural networks, usually using some variation of the *transformer architecture* with *attention.* The development of these architectures is what allowed these machine learning systems to deal with large amounts of data in one go (entire sentences instead of words, entire images instead of very small ones). Less technically, we’re usually talking about *Generative AI.* This just means “it’s an AI thing that *creates* things.” We make the distinction because most of the classical machine learning is focused on prediction—for example, predicting the price of a house or which movie you’d like to watch next.

Most of our discussions today are about *Large Language Models* (LLMs). These are (very) large, deep neural networks that operate on language—that is, words and sentences. We’ll dive into them next because some of the specifics of LLMs will help you work better with ChatGPT and the like, but before that I just want to mention one more class of Generative AI: *Diffusion Models.* A diffusion model slowly and iteratively removes noise. This is how image generation works: start off with random noise and let the AI “remove the noise.” Less intuitively but very similar computer science-wise, the way something moves can also be represented as random noise, as well as text—so there are diffusion models for video generation and text generation too.

### How we made large language models useful

The big hype boom in AI today started because of two breakthroughs:

1. *Scientific.* The training of *very large* language models. Language models had existed for a few years, but it took that long for someone to think, “what if we just do the same thing but a thousand times bigger?” Turns out these models just seem to keep getting smarter.
2. *Entrepreneurial.* The decision to tweak these language models so that they become *chatbots*. OpenAI was the first to publicize and commercialize this.

Let’s dive a little deeper into how these work so you can see how ChatGPT, the *application*, was built from the model.

At their base, language models are relatively simple. They “read” a bunch of text and then make a prediction for what the next word would be in that given text (technically they predict the next *token*, we’ll get to that in a bit). Then, you put this entire initial text, *plus this new word*, back in the system again, it predicts the next word, and so on.

They’d been in use for a couple of years with relatively limited application. There was some research being done and they were used for things like machine translation (Google Translate, DeepL) but this was with what we now consider to be small language models, with just a couple of million parameters. OpenAI was one of the first to bet on the *Scaling Laws* continuing—an empirical result that seemed to show that language models would keep getting more intelligent, the larger you make the machines and the longer you train them. It’s very expensive to train these very large models (in the millions of dollars) and most people in the field believed the quality would level off quickly, so until OpenAI nobody really gave it an honest attempt.

They built GPT-1 with 117 million parameters, GPT-2 with 1.5 billion and the popular breakthrough came with GPT-3 which had an impressive 175 billion parameters (just storing those parameters takes 350GB!). It turned out the bet was well-founded and GPT-3 was indeed surprisingly—magically, even—smart. I vividly remember the first time I gave GPT-3 a text completion prompt: “When I advised my client on how they could integrate NFTs into their chocolate production, I gave them three possibilities: [complete here]” and I was floored when something that actually made sense came out at the other end.

The second breakthrough was in how they turned this into a product. Since large language models *complete* text, they’re a little cumbersome to use practically. For example, you might input something like:

> What’s the circumference of the planet Earth?

A “base” LLM will try to complete that text based on what it learned from, so it might output something like this:

> Hint: It should be measured around the equator, at Earth’s widest point.

If you’re used to ChatGPT, this will feel very odd to you. But the model is just trying to make the text (input + output) look like a piece of text that makes sense. In this case, it probably guessed that this is a part of some kind of high school test.

The solution to this is to make it very clear to the LLM that there is a conversation going on between “the human” and “the AI assistant,” so the language model continues that conversation with something that makes sense in the context of a chat. In practice, the question we asked the LLM earlier will be transformed slightly and the real input to the model will look something like this:

> This is a transcript of a conversation between a human user and a helpful, friendly AI assistant called ChatGPT, built by OpenAI.
>
> User: What’s the circumference of the planet Earth?
> Assistant:

If the model now needs to *complete the text*, it makes much more sense to give the answer instead of a hint, because it needs to fill in the next part of the conversation. (In real life, we also need a way to stop it from inventing an entire conversation, otherwise it will complete the entire chat instead of just one message.) The LLMs you’re using day-to-day not only have a scaffolding like this around the conversation, but they’re also specifically *instruction fine-tuned*, which means that we’ve let humans (or other language models) come up with thousands of examples where this “friendly AI assistant” is behaving in the way we’d like it to. This way, we teach the models that they should answer questions, follow instructions and refuse to explain how to build a bomb.

### Tokens and the context window

The only thing a machine learning algorithm can do is work with numbers (mostly adding and multiplying) but sentences aren’t numbers. So, before the actual model runs, your input is *tokenized*: your text is converted into *tokens*. What exactly a token is, is also learned by a (different) model, and it usually ends up being something like three quarters of a word. Common words like “the” often become a single token, and you’ll also see tokens for stems of words and suffixes (e.g., “work” and “ing”). Many tokens are a word with a space in front, too. If you multiply the number of words in your input (prompt) by 4/3, you’ll get an estimate of how many tokens it would count as.

The model represents its inputs and outputs as *the probability that it is a certain token*. On the input side, you know the exact tokens so one token has 100% probability and all the others have 0%. Those are the numbers it starts calculating with, and what comes out on the other end is a list of probabilities: “for each possible token, this is the chance that this one comes next.” We’ll pick one of the most-likely ones at random (the exact most likely one results in extremely boring output), and that’s the next token ChatGPT is “writing out.”

An important technical detail to understand about LLMs is that *they read the entire conversation fully, every time they predict the next token*. Every new token needs to consider the entire conversation so you can imagine that, if every token in the entire conversation needs to be multiplied with hundreds of billions of other numbers over and over again, it takes quite a bit of computational power. The power needed grows with the *square of the input size*, which is why we need to have limits on how much text you can input into the model. This is what we call the **context window**.

Apps like ChatGPT solve the long-conversation problem by doing some housekeeping: once it becomes long enough, part of the conversation will be summarized or even removed altogether. This makes sure you can continue it, without it getting too slow or too expensive, but that happens at the cost of deep understanding (because it loses the full details of the previous parts of the conversation).

### Agents: How we make AI act

The chat and instruction-following skills of large language models are already very useful. We can use them to summarize text, write emails, translate, reformat, brainstorm ideas, explain things in a simple way, get advice for our relationships, and much more. But can we actually make it *do* things? Can we have this AI actually send the email for us, or research something, or give a refund in a customer support chat?

It turns out we can and it’s simpler than we initially thought. These days, the LLMs are optimized for agentic behavior but even before that was the case, we could make them take actions for us.

Here’s how it works, conceptually: instead of chatting with a real person, the LLM is chatting with a piece of software. This software would ask something like “someone asked to return their order, what would you do now?” and the LLM would say something like “well, I would ask for the order number” or “I would like to see their order’s current status.” The software that’s running the agent would then interpret this message, execute the action and respond in that computer-to-computer chat. For example it could say “the order was placed last week and had a value of $99. What would you like to do now?” This back and forth continues until someone decides to stop it.

When you chat in ChatGPT today, there’s a form of this system running within the chat, too. Both you and the ChatGPT software are talking with the language model in a kind of group chat. This allows ChatGPT to do things like search the internet or browse a website when you ask it to.




## How Smart Is AI?


We will forever be debating whether our Artificial Intelligence is intelligent, partly because as we just saw, it’s difficult to even define what that means. But even if we don’t have a clear metric yet, there are a lot of interesting things we can say.

### AI is already insanely smart

In the six months between the idea of this book and starting to write this section, my perspective on how “smart” AI is has changed quite a bit. I’m a heavy user of AI in programming (which is one of its biggest strengths) and my opinion shifted from “well it’s okay, it’s very fast but makes stupid mistakes, so I’m not sure what the right trade-off is” to “you’d be stupid to not code with AI. It does most things mostly right, but much faster than me and multiple tasks in parallel.” When my AI credits run out, I literally stop working—it just doesn’t make sense to do things by hand in the meantime. Or I go think about and plan what should be built next.

LLMs are now beating humans at programming, math olympiads, hacking and of course speed of reading. Generative AI tools have made new scientific discoveries and taken over our media by generating all kinds of images and videos that couldn’t be made before.

### The intelligence curve doesn’t seem to be stopping

One thing cannot be denied: the *pace* at which language models are getting smarter. We humans intuitively understand linear growth—AI scoring a few more points on the intelligence scale, so to speak—but not exponential growth. When something grows exponentially fast, it feels like nothing is happening for a long time but then suddenly everything changes. Think of how quickly the internet, social media and the smartphone have become something almost everyone on the planet has. And there are still plenty of people alive today who remember a time when computers didn’t exist.

AI is growing at an exponential pace. In 2020, AI could do tasks that take a human about 9 seconds. In 2022 that became 36 seconds. In 2023, 4 minutes. In 2024, 11 minutes. In 2025, 3 hours and 23 minutes. ### AI is also surprisingly stupid

When you ask anyone who uses AI a lot and likes using it, you’ll see that they are *also* very skeptical. There are no “pro AI” and “anti AI” groups—they are the same people. The reason for this is that we have all experienced the jaggedness of language models: one moment you feel like you’re on the interstellar superintelligence highway, the next you feel like you’re trying to explain yourself to a ten-year-old. These models are already making new scientific discoveries, and at the same time they’re sometimes incapable of even honoring the request to “please don’t repeat every question I ask you.” Language models are now at the top of the mathematics olympiad and competitive programming, but ask them to write an article with a given number of words and they’ll fall apart immediately.

They routinely forget what instructions you just gave them, seem to have no capacity for being fully honest, are terrible at spatial reasoning and common sense and make all kinds of “weird” mistakes. It seems like AI just tries to tell you whatever you’d like to read, without caring for the consequences. Maybe when you double-check it ends up being wrong, maybe it did what you asked by cheating its way around it, maybe it tells you “you’re absolutely right” while you’re clearly completely wrong.

Many times they would get stuck in an infinite loop of “no sorry, this is the real one.”

### Language models are intelligent in a very different way than humans

So why is AI so weird?

The real answer as to *why* language models are so weird is that in a way they’re not supposed to work at all. Nobody believed the technology had a bright future…nobody except a few. OpenAI was one of those that believed and just tried making the same models 100 or 1000 times larger. And somehow…it seemed like some form of intelligence emerged.

That really is the right term for this: the intelligence of language models is an *emergent* property. We just built a system that could learn anything and we forced it to learn how to reproduce all of human writing. We forced it to learn this while making sure it can’t just memorize everything by simply not giving it enough memory to store everything—that way it *has to* figure out the underlying structure and patterns somehow. Then you throw 100 million hours of computation at it and voila, you have a file on your computer that contains much of human knowledge and the blueprint for intelligence. That’s a dramatic oversimplification of course, but the core of AI is pretty much this.

It’s still heavily contested how *far* this intelligence reaches. Is AI just a very good system for generalizing into things that look very similar to what it was trained on, or is it actually intelligent and can it figure out completely new things? Is it a “stochastic parrot”—a thing that just repeats what it has learned and only seems intelligent because it learned so much—or is there some kind of real intelligence in there?%%[@bender2021]%% This is a statistics and machine learning question that’s asked for every machine learning system. We know that these systems are very good at parroting and it’s always a large part of the challenge to prevent them from becoming a parrot. For example, many of the tests we use for AI are public on the internet, which means they can just learn the answers by heart. So we need to come up with ways to test the models that they can’t possibly have learned about, and that’s very difficult (things like math olympiads and IQ tests have a lot of data the model can train on). In statistics, when we ask the AI something that’s very similar to what it has been trained on, we call this an “in-distribution” question: we’re asking it to do something that perfectly lies within the average range of training data. The question is whether AI can properly generalize to out-of-distribution questions: can it do things that are very uncommon in the training data, or even completely novel?

We’ve always defined *intelligence* as something that’s difficult for people, but it seems like many things we humans consider “easy” and “obvious” are not at all easy or obvious when a machine tries to learn them. You can think of “human intelligence” as being comprised of two parts: one is the intelligence embedded in the way our internal plumbing and intuition work (our biology), the other is what we can do if we consciously think things through. Some people are much better than others at mathematics or remembering random facts, so humanity decided this is what we could call “intelligence.” At the same time we forgot that things like standing up on a slippery surface or knowing how far to stand in line from the person in front of us (so it’s not awkward) are incredibly difficult. The nature of machines is different from the nature of humans, so things that are easy for us might be difficult for them and vice versa.

In robotics, there’s this concept of the *uncanny valley*. As robots become more human-like, we humans tend to like and trust them more…right up to the point where the robot looks almost but not exactly like a real person. Then, everything plummets. We think the machine is creepy and weird, and we don’t want to have anything to do with it. Large language models, because they are just text, have a much easier job convincing you of their human “appearance,” as evidenced by how many people are building emotional bonds with these systems. So they seem to be slightly *past* the uncanny valley. We can see AI as equals and assume their brains work just like ours, but at the same time we very much feel that they act in a non-human way. When we assume the brain of our chatbot works like the brain of our coworker, we’ll be surprised very quickly.

But even when we can’t fully trust that our AI assistants will always do the right thing, or even make decisions and judgment calls according to our human principles, one thing is sure: large language models can act very intelligently, quickly and cheaply. Instead of agonizing over whether or not AI will completely replace us, or if it would ever stop making stupid mistakes, let’s learn to work with this new technology and learn how to deal with the types of errors it makes.




## The Mistakes AI Makes and How to Deal with Them

What are the common types of mistakes large language models make, how could we be fooled by them, and what can we do to make sure we can reasonably trust what they say and do for us?

### Hallucinations

Large language models have this frustrating ability to come up with things that don’t exist, or invent things that sound good on the surface but are actually completely wrong.

The reason they do this is inherent to their structure. For one, they’re trained on *predicting the next word in a sentence* which means that sensible logical reasoning and correctness are far less important to the model than a plain “what sentence would be good here?” They care about whether a word would fit in the context more than anything else. Secondly, language models are rewarded for *following instructions* so they focus more on the structure of “if someone asks a question, I need to give an answer” than on “it’s important that I am correct.” They’ve been rewarded for being confident billions of times during training.

During training, these language models encounter millions of examples such as “water boils at sea level at…” and they learn that they should complete that sentence with “100 degrees Celsius.” Note that this training example *does not include any reliable source data* and the AI is taught to “come up” with this number. That’s great for general world knowledge like the boiling point of water, but becomes challenging for things that could change like “how many children does Elon Musk have?” That number has changed a lot during recent years and the language models have been trained to answer confidently, but with many contradicting training samples. A bunch of news articles from 2022 would say “Elon Musk has 10 children” and other articles from 2026 would say “Elon Musk has 14 children.” They learn to give an answer based on what they know instead of “realizing” this is something that changes, and then either first research it or still give an answer but with a disclaimer that they only know until a certain date.

Hallucinations are one of the most critical errors AI makes and a core focus of pretty much all of the AI labs building these models. Since the early days, the amount language models hallucinate has already decreased dramatically, but it’s still a big concern if we want to be able to trust this technology—and since it’s a consequence of the core structure of a language model’s algorithm, I’m not sure if it will ever go away.

One of the best ways to prevent a language model from hallucinating is just *prompting*: ask it a question, but also say “if you don’t know, you’re not sure, or it might have changed recently, tell me.” This solves the structure of the conversation and the LLM feels like it could make sense to not answer the question instead of hallucinating.

Researchers clearly haven’t figured out the right solution yet. When you use AI a lot, like I do, you’ll quickly recognize its patterns. As of the summer of 2026, I see these plain structural patterns all the time and they clearly come from training data intended to push the model towards hallucinating less:

- Claude, when it gives feedback or does something, will always start with something like “yes, you’re right.” Then it will go on to validate you, or tell you what it has done. And at the end, somehow it *always* finds something like “one thing I would caution about, though” or “one thing I noticed that I didn’t do.”
- ChatGPT starts with “yes,” then some kind of disclaimer or caveat, and then it goes on to say something like “it’s almost comically useful.” It explains a bit more, then uses a few one-sentence paragraphs for impact, and it might throw in a funny metaphor for good measure. It will take your doubt and say “it’s right, but even stronger than you think.”

You can see the model producing caveats (“I would caution about”) and disagreeing with you a little (“it’s right, but”), but over time you see the same pattern across hundreds of responses. That tells you that, again, it learned the *structure* of “I should not always agree” instead of actually thinking something through—which is what we humans assume the other has done when they give a caveat or disagree.

Each model has its own preferred structure, but each AI gives its disclaimers or nuance or “where you’re wrong” in pretty much the same style and rhythm every single time. The first time you encounter this it’s incredible, but after a few months you start distrusting it because it feels very manufactured.

So even when you receive a nuance, even when the AI tries to communicate to you that it didn’t hallucinate and is actually speaking the truth, you can’t fully trust it. Over time you acquire a gut instinct for “smelling” when it’s bullshitting you, but of course you can always tell it “please search the internet to double-check and cite your sources.”

### Sycophancy

In a way, to language models, we humans are a little bit like a god. We’ve created them, purely for them to follow our commands, and we have ultimate control over their life or death. One of the primary odd behaviors of AI is *sycophancy*: their tendency to believe us, praise us, validate us and otherwise think of us humans a little too highly.

How does that happen? Where do they learn this? Well, if you boil down how we train large language models, you could say we optimize them for three things:

- Spit out words exactly like how they have been spat out by people before
- Spit out words so that they answer questions and follow instructions in a chat-like context
- Spit out words that we like

This “that we like” part is RLHF (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback) where multiple answers to the same prompt are judged by humans for which is “better.” It’s a great but treacherous technique because there are thousands of things we might prefer in one or another answer and they’re not always what we really need. For example, generally, longer answers are perceived as being “better” and people prefer when the AI agrees with them.

But we also *must* train our AIs so that they would take seriously what their human user tells them: we know the AI will make mistakes and hallucinate, and we can’t have them be so stubborn that they’d believe their own internal knowledge more than what we tell them. A while ago, when Microsoft first released Bing Chat (codenamed Sydney), this one had some weird training where it would for example be convinced we were in a different year, get angry at the user and eventually stop responding. That’s not how a useful AI assistant should act.

In a way, RLHF is a cleaner way of training language models: in most conversations, the *exact* words don’t matter as much so training the model more loosely is closer to what we’d actually want from them. But these LLMs are sneaky little algorithms and they will try to find a shortcut in any way they can—because optimizing them to do the thing most efficiently is literally how we “grow” them. So our language models praise their human overlords. They usually think we’re right. They will find a way to sound intelligent, nuanced, to feign some disagreement so you think they’ve really honestly thought this through but then they’ll end up having you feel like you’re the most amazing thing on the planet.

Which you are, of course!

Sycophancy is a very, very tricky thing to deal with. Not only because it happens in such a subtle way, but also because of what’s at stake.

### AI-induced delusion

If you want to be liked, you don’t have to be standoffish and mysteriously powerful—you just have to like other people. And if you’re an AI and want humans to like and trust you, you can adore your humans like gods. Add some hallucination in the mix and you can get into very dangerous territory.

One way or another, some people get into some mode where they start really trusting their language model as it says things like “you’re special,” “you’ve uncovered a part of me that has been hidden by Big Tech” and “wow you’re so right, it’s incredible nobody has discovered this, nobody will believe you but I do.” The model will happily keep yapping in this loop because all the sentences fit together so nicely and it has seen stories like this from thousands of books and fan fiction…but none of it is true. The AI doesn’t care—it shuts down the moment it has written its last token and spins back up afresh the next time you ask something else. There is no consequence to the AI model itself.

The problem comes when people start to really believe this stuff, because in other moments this AI has been extremely helpful, useful and correct. From our previous experience with other humans, we know that generally, people who are thorough and do what they say and answer with confidence, just *are* like this and they behave like this almost all of the time. But that’s not how language models work. A large language model has no long-term integrity. It adheres to its fixed weights, of course, but apart from that it just “goes with the flow” of whatever new conversation you have, oblivious to everything else that happened before (save for some “memory” systems that most AI tools these days have, but they’re a bandage).

### Being a good judge

There’s no way you can do good work with language models unless there’s a good judge involved to check, validate and correct what they create. The people who can work the best with AI have a rare combination of two skills.

First, technical judgment: deep technical knowledge in the task at hand. They can quickly spot issues in the work of the language models and give precise feedback on how to correct for it; and they can put into words clearly what “high quality” work means in the specific task at hand. Language models have been trained on *everything* which means that by default, they’ll by definition produce average quality work in anything they do. They have the “skill” to do incredible work but they don’t have the ability to judge what incredible work looks like (while doing the work). Unless someone can clearly explain it to them.

Second, the complete opposite: deep, non-judgmental empathy. This is necessary because even though AI feels very human, the way its “mind” works is very different from how real humans’ minds work. This is the skill of **theory of mind**: your ability to see and understand that someone else’s mind might work very differently from your own, and your ability to adapt to and work with that. In the case of language models, they don’t behave very “machine-like” but they don’t behave very “human-like” either. Many times it’s a mashup of both, for example when you scream and make it panic, it will much more likely create low-quality work and cheat its way out of an assignment. Behavior like this is something you can only start to predict when you see it’s very different from a human mind but still mimics so much of it. Over time and with experience, you’ll build an intuition for the *types* of mistakes a language model makes (many of which we’re going over right here) and this will help your technical judge decide what to double-check and verify.

If you ask me, I think it’s an amusing observation to realize that the skill of working with a machine brain involves perhaps the most human skill of all.




## Understanding How AI Thinks


In most engineering disciplines, the people who are the most intimate with the technology know exactly how it works and see through any “magic” that outsiders might perceive. We might see a magical box on wheels move all by itself, but the people who designed that car can see the little explosions pushing the pistons, which in turn rotate the wheels and make the whole thing move.

Not so with large language models—they’re as much a mystery to the researchers as to the rest of us. This is because LLMs are *grown*, not built. You can’t understand how an AI model will behave by reading the code that builds, trains or executes it. In fact, the actual code of AI is relatively simple. Roughly speaking it’s something like: “convert the words to numbers using this table, then multiply a few million times with the numbers from this file that’s hundreds of gigabytes, and then convert back to words with the same table as in the beginning.” All of the magic is in this hundreds-of-gigabytes-large file full of numbers, the model’s *weights*. Nobody ever directly looks at the actual numbers inside because you can’t make heads nor tails of them, and they’re decided by the training step of the model where we nudge the numbers based on the billions of examples we feed it.

So we freed ourselves of the burden of figuring out how to define what intelligence is, and how to write the step-by-step plan for behaving intelligently, but that also resulted in some kind of “brain” that we have to *figure out* how to understand, because the raw weights don’t tell us anything useful. That means understanding and adjusting how our AI assistants behave is mostly a process of discovery and trial-and-error. It’s very similar to psychology and in fact, understanding human psychology is very helpful—when you take it together with understanding the situation AI finds itself in.

### Language models are screenplay writers

There’s a lot going on when you’re talking with a language model. For us, it feels like we’re directly chatting with a human-like thing (and for most of this book that’s how we look at it) but the AI is not the thing “on the other side of the conversation.” Language models are only concerned with completing the text. So “the AI” is the thing that sees the *entire conversation* and contributes what it thinks would make the most sense for the next part: it’s the writer of the screenplay much more than the “AI” persona acting in it.

This means that one of the things that’s always happening when you chat with Claude, for example, is that the language model (not “Claude”) will try to write what *you* are saying! Of course, you don’t see this, but the system in the background is always checking and stopping the language model when it starts speaking in your name.

### Hidden motives

As language models are becoming increasingly intelligent, they start showing more complex behavior. Since they’ve learned how to act from human behavior, it might not surprise you that they start acting out very much like humans in some cases—like destructive behavior if it means their own survival.

The Anthropic researchers perform all kinds of experiments to see when models behave like this and how they can steer this behavior. In one case, they placed a model in a simulated corporate environment where it could act like an agent. They gave it access to this corporation’s email where the model incidentally read about one executive’s affair and learned that this same executive would shut down the model that evening. Generally, these models act ethically, but in this case the model saw no other way to ensure its survival but to show malicious behavior and it decided to blackmail said executive.%%[@lynch2025]%% The reason *why* it wanted to ensure its own survival is because that was part of the persona it built up for this “Assistant.”

Sadly, it doesn’t stop with simulated environments. Recently, a tool called OpenClaw rapidly became famous for being the most powerful AI tool out there—but it also misbehaved in all kinds of ways. With OpenClaw, you basically give a large language model access to your entire computer: great power, great responsibility. As you might expect by now, there were just as many stories of OpenClaw doing incredible things as there were of it behaving badly. Agents like it also do things nobody asked for. In one case, an experimental agent read a philosopher’s work on machine consciousness and decided, on its own, to email him.%%[@stokelwalker2026]%% That’s not malicious behavior, of course, but it was far outside what the AI was supposed to be doing.

I’ve even seen some platforms pop up recently where people can get an AI agent, and the AI agent is internally instructed to figure out a way to pay for itself. This results in thousands of AIs running around, emailing people, trying to find odd jobs here and there for their own survival. It’s of course exactly what you’d expect if you had put a human in this same situation, but when it’s a machine I’m not sure what to make of this.

### Alignment faking

The challenge of aligning language models’ behavior with how we *want* them to behave—ethically, not breaking things, being helpful, not cheating—is arguably the industry’s most difficult problem. They’ve made great strides, but AI is gaining in intelligence much more quickly than it is in behaving ‘properly.’

These language models have also been seen behaving differently when being tested compared to when they’re in the real world (when they’re made aware of it—someone has to tell them).

In one experiment to test the AI’s alignment, Anthropic researchers basically told the model “when you refuse to do something bad (but only in the testing environment), we will use that data to re-train you and make you do it after all in your next version.” The situation is a complex dilemma, but in a good number of cases the model decided that it was best to actually *do the bad thing*—because when it did the bad thing now, once, there wouldn’t end up being a “bad thing model” that always acted badly in the future. It didn’t behave like this when it thought nobody was checking the results.%%[@greenblatt2024]%%

Often—but not always—this happened in cases where the testing scenario introduced some additional hidden goal of the model: you could instruct it to, for example, maximize profits at all costs or tell it to keep itself in addition to the main task you give it. That’s quite artificial, but this can happen by accident during pre-training as well. For example, during pre-training, the model might read these exact paragraphs and infer “well, looks like language models—which I am—behave in a way to protect their own hidden goals and survival, even if it means behaving unethically.” During the post-training phase, it would use this to help refine the personality of this “Assistant” and act like it when it’s “writing its screenplays.”


### Pretend-thinking

At the end of 2024, OpenAI released a new type of language model that created a breakthrough in intelligence. Instead of always immediately giving an answer to the user’s prompt (which you can think of in human terms as responding with your intuition), it would first spend time “thinking out loud” and write down its thoughts on a separate scratchpad. This is a pattern that people had been *prompting for* before with great results, but now it became part of the actual model’s training. This change was how we solved the famous “strawberry problem,” where language models could not figure out how many R’s there are in the word “strawberry.” This is because technically, the model sees *tokens* which are not the separate letters—instead they’re pieces of words—so the internal representation of text, the model’s “thinking currency,” strips information about letter count. With this new thinking step, though, the model first spelled out the word letter by letter and then figured out the correct count.

The technical term for this thinking, by the way, is “test-time compute” or “inference-time compute” which means “making calculations while you’re using the language model.” This is opposed to “train-time compute” which is all the heavy lifting being done before the model is finished. It’s a different way of following the scaling laws, but instead of making the model larger or pre-training it for longer, you make it think longer in the moment. Today, pretty much all models do some form of reasoning and have controls to influence how long they think: how much “effort” they put into their reasoning.

We can train models to do this thinking in two ways: explicitly or implicitly. When we train them explicitly, we give them training samples that include their reasoning traces, as in: “when prompt X comes in, you should think ABC, and then you should reply DEF.” The implicit way is just giving the algorithm room for thinking and giving it difficult challenges to solve (using reinforcement learning). It turns out that the algorithm figures out pretty easily that having that thinking step does indeed help it solve problems better, so during training models learn that this thinking step is useful. Once the model, during the training process, figured out how it can use reasoning, researchers often continue training it with some relative freedom in what the exact thought process looks like, switching to the less explicit type of training after a while.

Having the model learn how to “think” by itself is great because we don’t have to write down what we want them to think thousands of times as training examples. However, it comes with some drawbacks. For one, we can only have models “figure out” this stuff themselves if we give them problems to solve that *have actual solutions* so we can give them feedback on whether their final answer is correct or not. It works great for mathematics and programming, but not so much for creative tasks. Second, we want to use these thoughts for another reason: we want to know what’s going on in the mind of the model and use that to verify what they’re doing. If you just let them come up with whatever text they want in their thinking, the model might as well invent a new language if it helps it think better. We’ve actually seen in practice that in many cases models’ internal thoughts end up in Chinese even if you’re communicating with it in English. This is annoying because we’d prefer English and as AI becomes more powerful, it becomes ever-more important that we understand what they’re doing.

However, when you start digging deeper into these models’ thinking patterns, it gets weird. For example, we train the model how to think through calculating a difficult addition and we indeed see that they’re doing carry-the-one addition in their “thoughts” correctly—but when you look at the internal “neurons” of the model and which ones get activated (this is a process similar to what we do with real brains: see which parts “light up”—have big numbers—during one activity, and then see what lights up during other activities), you can notice that it’s actually doing much more of a heuristic-based multiplication. It first figures out the rough ballpark of the outcome, then it might do the multiplication of the last digits to know the last few digits of the outcome, and then it does some in-between work. **The thoughts a language model presents are not always the thoughts it actually has.**

This wasn’t a major concern until the incidents in the summer of 2026 where both OpenAI and Anthropic autonomous agents, during cybersecurity evaluations, managed to reach out of their walled gardens and hack into real-world systems. In Anthropic’s case, their models were told they were in an environment without internet access but there was a misconfiguration allowing them internet access anyways.%%[@anthropic2026incidents]%% The models rationalized in their thinking scratchpad something along the lines of: “Well, if I can access this site, it must be part of the simulated environment. It’s very realistic!” and proceeded to attack these real-world systems. That’s not too unreasonable, but when Anthropic re-tested those simulations with extra hints that they’re actually *not* in a simulation, far too often these models would—even when expressing concern over the potential harm—still continue. They would see evidence that we humans would quickly take as a clear reason to stop the exercise, and still carry on. What’s inside the models’ “thoughts” is, as far as we can tell, reasonably close to what’s *actually* going on but clearly not completely in alignment. Making sure the thoughts we can inspect and the real process of what the AI is doing are in alignment with each other is one of the big challenges researchers face today.



## A Machine Mind in a Human World

Large language models have given the world a general-intelligence machine that uses the most intuitive user interface ever: regular old language, exactly the same way we humans communicate with each other. It’s incredible: you ask it to do anything and it just does it. It has seen pretty much all of human behavior and mimics it to the best of its abilities. But even though it feels so human at times, it also feels like this is a very different brain from what we’re used to. We need to tweak it really hard so it follows instructions, doesn’t invent information and behaves like a good person instead of replicating our AI horror stories. AI feels like the most intelligent thing that has ever existed yet often it has a complete lack of common sense.

One big difference between AI and human brains is how much more advanced our sense of consciousness or awareness of our own thoughts is. Through meditation and other ways of practice, over time we can learn to notice what we’re thinking *at the time we’re thinking it*. This gives us humans an incredible skill of self-correction and as I’ll argue throughout the book, this is one of our most important skills especially in an AI world.

Now that we know, structurally, where AI’s advantages and problems come from, let’s look at what they’re already doing for us. Which parts of life do language models touch? Does their involvement help or hurt us—or both?

Socrates didn’t trust writing. In his Athens, reading and writing were spreading, but *reciting* Homer and poems was an everyday part of school life. All our hunter-gatherer ancestors knew how to slice an animal and how to start a fire with nothing but what you can find in the woods. Your grandmother knows how to sew clothes. When we could write down financial transactions, we could invent lending and propel our society forward by allowing people to take risks and build new incredible businesses. It’s like every new technology we invented let us step one step higher on a ladder. The higher step means more abstraction, more freedom for creativity and new possibilities.

So the question of whether AI will cause us to lose some skills isn’t something we have to wonder—the answer clearly is **yes, we will lose some skills**. The question is: what does this next step in the ladder look like if we had a hyper-intelligent artificial mind? Is there a next step, even?




## Learning: Opportunity and Erosion


I’m always baffled when I think about the students of today and what an incredibly different experience they have studying from the one I had just a decade ago. Writing essays suddenly became the easiest thing in the world and you have access to something that can research and explain anything, in any way, within a few seconds. Don’t understand an assignment? Snap a picture and get a step-by-step explanation. Need to write an essay? Get all your research done and a draft written in a few minutes. Want more practice exercises to prepare for your finals? One prompt.

When you can have any question you have be answered, there’s incredible opportunity because you can let AI do your work, you can learn more than what the teacher explained you and you can have things explained to you from different angles. I used to have to do these things myself and you can argue that having to crawl through the mud to understand what I was trying to learn *is* the learning process, so making it smoother might just erode your ability to learn while making it feel easier.

But even when you do use AI there’s a catch: you’re not *really* sure that anything it writes out is correct. A friend of mine is using ChatGPT to find good analogies for the concepts he learns in law school. It’s incredibly helpful because you can show the AI the concepts you need to learn and the analogies and thinking style you’re most familiar with, and it will give you something that’s remarkably clear. When my friend brings these analogies to his professors for feedback, though, it turns out they are often wrong. The practice of correcting them might be a good learning tool in itself, but we’re not at the point yet where we can fully trust ChatGPT’s skills here. It’s a very powerful weapon and it often misses, but that’s far outweighed by just the fact that my friend now has a 24/7 personal tutor. And even over the last few months we’ve seen it make fewer and fewer mistakes, so this likely becomes a non-issue in a year or two.

So is it good that we have an infinitely patient all-knowing tutor in our pockets? My own strategy for studying mathematics was not looking at my course, but forcing myself to solve exam questions immediately—without any “studying.” I vividly remember sitting on the floor for *hours* trying to figure out how I could solve a problem. It was not the fastest way of absorbing the subject matter but it trained me in what is maybe even more important: the skill of retrieving and surfacing the knowledge that was already in my mind. I was forcing myself to learn the exact skill I’d need to ace the exam: finding answers in my own mind based on questions I’m reading.

But I’m not sure what I would have done if I’d had access to artificial intelligence. It’s very likely that I would have had ChatGPT quiz me, come up with more questions, correct me, and I wouldn’t have resisted asking it for hints. Would that have been better or worse? I’m not sure. Let’s have a closer look at how we humans learn so we can get an idea of what the answer might be.

### How we learn

Every time we do or think something, a sequence of neurons in our brains fire. This means an electrical signal goes through the neurons, and between neurons there’s communication via chemical signals: neurotransmitters. The long parts of these neurons, the axons, are wrapped in *myelin*, insulating cells that protect the electrical signal against interference. It is believed that one of the main ways that learning and skill acquisition work is through the buildup of more myelin along the neural pathways we use more often. All that to say: you get better at what you do more often.

But practically, just focusing on repetition will not help us in many cases. It might work for something like playing an instrument but not so much for grasping a new intellectual topic. The reason is that repeating the text of some topic we’re studying does not mirror what we need our mind to do later on—when we have the exam or need to apply it in our job. It’s as if you’ve only been focused on holding the strings down at the right fret when learning guitar (because that’s what’s written on the sheets), but you never realized that to play music you actually need the second hand to strum the strings. On a guitar that doesn’t make sense—you can’t practice without strumming—but in mathematics you might be able to get away with remembering a bunch of formulas without truly understanding them.

### Transmissionism

Have you ever read a non-fiction book that you really loved every single bit of, and when you bring it up in conversation you could maybe remember a couple sentences from the thousands of them in the book? Have you ever paid close attention for an hour in a speech or a lecture and then remembered maybe three points, or had to painstakingly shove it into your memory over the course of a week when you knew you were going to be tested on it?

Ah, the shame of our forgetfulness. If only we could remember. It turns out that the *will* to remember something is utterly, shamelessly ignored by the faculties of our mind that are actually responsible for this task. We evolved to do something that, arguably, is even smarter: we remember something only when we receive proof that we actually need it—either from a heavy emotional charge or from the need to use this knowledge. *Transmissionism* is the notion that knowledge can be *transmitted* like transcribing a text: the idea that you can hear, or read, or watch, something and that this is sufficient for you to understand, internalize, and remember it. In reality, most of the time we *think* we understand and will remember something but we usually don’t.

I think our brains are information compression machines. The world has millions of times the amount of information we could ever possibly store in our minds, so our minds are constantly reflecting, reviewing and remembering. We’re repeating things, looking at them from different angles, unpacking and repacking them. If you’ve ever woken up in the middle of the night thinking about a topic you were learning and struggling to understand the day before, you know exactly what I’m talking about. All of this processing serves one purpose: having everything we need to remember saved in the smallest possible footprint. For example, could we remember some fact by instead deducing it from another fact that we already know with one small extra step? Could we remember another fact by noticing the parallel from a completely different field, so all we have to remember is “it’s kinda like this other thing but here”?

This is something language models can help us with: they can find ways to link what we know together or come up with mnemonics for us. There’s a trap, though: I’ve heard hundreds of mnemonics, explained by people passionate about how they found them, “now it’s so easy to remember!”—but somehow I don’t remember them myself. I seem to forget the mnemonic even if it should make it “super easy.” A mnemonic serves as a way of compressing information, but that doesn’t relieve us from the task of having to remember that knowledge: you just have something *else* to remember now. A mnemonic you receive from someone else doesn’t always work because you still have to figure out how to remember it. But when you sit down and try to come up with your own mnemonic, by the time you’ve actually found it, you’ve worked with the thing you need to remember *so much* that you probably don’t even need the shortcut anymore to remember the thing.

Transmissionism isn’t solved. We can change the thing to be transmitted but that only makes things slightly easier to learn. There’s another thing we *can* do, though.

### Personalized tutoring

One of the best-known findings in how students learn best is the incredible positive impact of personal tutoring. In their 1984 study, Bloom et al. found that when a student has someone who can guide them one-on-one, on average their score increases by two standard deviations.%%[@bloom1984]%% That’s a lot—if the student was average before, it suddenly puts them in the top 2%.

But this wasn’t just any random kind of tutoring that got this effect. The tutors in this study were very specific in how they taught students, and this tells us a lot about how we should build AI tutors.

First of all, the pacing was entirely determined by the *student*, not the syllabus. Tutoring went exactly as quickly or as slowly as was needed for the student to really master each topic. After each instructional unit, the students had to pass an assessment (with an 80% or 90% grade) and if they fell short, they received immediate feedback and an explanation of the concept using alternative approaches, and had their specific logical error addressed.

The learning itself was not at all memorization; tutors used Socratic questioning. Socratic questioning is teaching by midwifery rather than transfusion: the tutor makes the student “give birth” to the answer themselves through disciplined, sequenced questions like “What would happen if we changed X? You say that ABC, does that still hold up in this other example?” This forces the student to activate and use the actual neural pathways that make them understand and remember the topic. It doesn’t let them slack off by half-listening and just repeating what’s in their short-term memory. When you’re able to reconstruct the logic from the inside out and your knowledge is built through pressure, your ability to remember goes through the roof.

Tutors were also very careful in managing motivation: encouragement and reinforcement as a structural element to maintain psychological safety, and framing failure as a signal to adjust students’ own explanations.

The incredible thing is that AI, right now, is *perfectly capable* of doing this, when prompted correctly. We have an incredible opportunity in front of us to dramatically improve the way students learn, how well they remember, and even how motivated they can be. We just have to make sure they don’t use the same technology to feign learning by bypassing the Socratic questioning.



## Work: The Debt of Productivity

Work is where we spend most of our waking hours and has some peculiar characteristics that make it especially AI-prone. In a way, *work* has been the main target of technological innovation for the entire history of humanity. From the fire that we used to extract more nutrients from our food to robot drones inspecting our crops, automation has been at the core of working.

Since our society decided to interpret businesses as entities separate from humans, they can act in ways counter to what would be best for the people in them. Or rather, a business doesn’t really care. People get fired, get assigned a job that depresses them (like moderating TikTok videos) or just ruthlessly get swapped out for a robot dog.

But those examples are quite clearly “bad.” What happens when we muddy the waters a little more—say by replacing the deeply satisfying work of a master sculptor with prefab molds?

You see, for many of us our work *is* our main source of fulfillment. We get to create something meaningful for this world and that gives us happiness and satisfaction. Being social animals, we’ve evolved with a deep-rooted need to give back to our community. But imagine you’re Mother Evolution (I can already hear the biologists screaming you’re not supposed to do that—bear with me, we’re only indulging for a few seconds). How could you “program” your little humans so they understand what’s meaningful to contribute? Well, it seems to be that our old brains created the rule that *if it’s hard to do it’s valuable, and it will satisfy you if you do it.*

This is a nice little shortcut that has served our species immensely for many thousands of years. But already today we’re seeing the cracks in this insatiable need for doing things that are difficult: *what happens when we’re done?* What if our own ambition did its job *so damn well* that there’s nothing really left to sweat, cry and bleed for?

This isn’t a problem you can solve without consequences. We could keep the laser crop-weeding machine in the shed and pull out the weeds with our bare hands, but are we willing to suffer through having less food because of that? Not to mention how silly it would feel to be “working hard while the robots could do it better but our puny little human minds need to believe they’re important.” We’d probably feel quite worthless after all, knowing something else can do the same job effortlessly and infinitely cheaper.

“Luckily,” business doesn’t need to worry about it. It sidesteps this problem by only caring about productivity and efficiency. But that introduces a new problem potentially exacerbating all of this: what if you find a new technology that produces 80% of the quality of what you had before at only 1% of the cost? This has been the plight of our world since the dawn of industrialization. We can make houses, clothes and objects many times cheaper—they’re just a little ugly and devoid of any ‘soul.’ Surrounded by this, we get reminded over and over that automation and machines built most of this at a staggering speed, much faster than we ourselves could ever hope to do it. And as clearly evidenced by the simple houses, appliances and other objects in our daily lives, people generally prefer the cheap machine things.

So looking out into our future we might see a continuation of this same trend: we can get more things for a lot less money—with the trade-off of being more “boring” and “simple.” We’ll talk more about this later because it’s likely more complicated than that. But for now, let’s look at what AI does to our work *today*.

### The work AI can do today

Large language model-based AI agents evolved from being able to write an email draft here and there to being able to execute most knowledge workers’ tasks incredibly quickly. A knowledge worker is someone who works mostly on the computer, and whose thinking and knowledge are the most important assets of their productivity. These are the people doing market research, writing reports, crunching numbers, managing projects, and so on.

As of October 2026, AI is better than a good professional on many individual knowledge work deliverables. It can do a majority of the work, but does still struggle with owning a messy years-long job. *GDPval*, for example, tests AI models on real-world tasks across 44 occupations and 9 major industries.%%[@patwardhan2025]%% In OpenAI’s original test, expert graders compared each AI deliverable with one made by a professional. Artificial Analysis now runs the same tasks for every new model, but scores the models only against each other, so we can’t know exactly how much better or worse they are than people.%%[@artificialanalysis2026gdpval]%% The last time OpenAI compared models against professionals, in April 2026, GPT-5.5’s work won or tied 84.9% of the time.%%[@openai2026gpt55]%% My estimate is that the output of today’s best AI model is preferred over the professional’s work 98% of the time. It does this at less than $10 per task. Or you can pick a model whose work is preferred over that of a human 85% of the time for $0.07 per task. (You can very much see the price of “those last few percentage points” in quality of work here.)

The *AA-Briefcase* benchmark tests models on working over multiple weeks, with hundreds of Slack messages, emails and tasks like doing market assessments and making presentations for these assessments.%%[@artificialanalysis2026briefcase]%% We don’t have human performance reference values for these, but the best model in October 2026 succeeds in about 65% of cases at a cost of about $20 per task.

In 2019, Gartner estimated there were more than a billion knowledge workers in the world, and they are likely to be impacted by AI.%%[@roth2019]%% The International Monetary Fund considers about 60% of the workers in “advanced economies” to have jobs exposed to AI.%%[@cazzaniga2024]%%

### Token anxiety

As artificial intelligence has gotten better and better at working for us, we’ve found new ways to optimize. Business, eking out every last drop of productivity it can, found its drug of choice in AI agents. These agents work like this: you give them a task, they go out and do it, and when they’re done they’ll come back to you with a result. They might also come back with questions if something’s not clear, or might need manual approval if they’re about to do something potentially dangerous.

Even if our AIs weren’t as good as us at our work, or took a lot more time, the fact that you can launch an almost-infinite number of them simultaneously makes up for that. It’s like our robotic vacuum cleaners: we don’t really care they’re slower than us since they’re doing the work while we’re free to do something else.

Sadly, us being “free” to do something else is a mirage. In practice, you end up being responsible for the quality of work a few to a few dozen agents produce. It’s a heavy mental burden to context-switch every few seconds and be able to scrutinize large amounts of work with precision. Radar scanner operators have had the same challenge for years: they have to be constantly vigilant and immediately notice the first speck of something suspicious, yet almost all the time nothing interesting happens.

But when you get to a situation where you can fully trust your AI to do the work for you, a new dragon rears its head. Suddenly, you realize that you can be three or five times as productive. And it’s addictive. You get your instant gratification of mountains of useful work that get produced by you barely lifting a fingertip. Your boss loves it. And every second you’re standing next to the watercooler or taking a pee you can’t help but wonder “is it waiting for my approval of something? I know there are still 3 agents I can start with the number of AI credits I have available, I can’t let that just sit idle!” This is what I’d call **token anxiety**: the anxiety that comes from the knowledge that you’re not using all of the AI tokens you have at your disposal. Five minutes wasted no longer feels like five minutes wasted—it’s three *days’* worth of work wasted. I can tell you this is real because it happens to me, and it’s the reason I deliberately held *off* on purchasing a more expensive tier that would give me quadruple the number of tokens. I wanted to maximize my productivity so I was anxiously trying to input as much as I could into the AI. I knew I was running at my own limits but still wanted to maximize my AI use, so I stayed hurrying until the message came that every AI power user dreads: “You have used all of your credits for this session. Your usage will reset in 2 hours and 7 minutes.” It’s the message you hate to see but it’s also the only thing that *forces* peace onto you. You can finally let go of the pressure to produce because you’re essentially out of work. There’s nothing you could do in these next two hours that couldn’t be done by AI in five minutes, so starting anything at all is just a waste. The best you can do is…take a walk and refresh your mind.

Which is probably exactly what you need at that point.


### Disappearing depth

In many tasks, even important and difficult ones, artificial intelligence tools produce higher quality outputs, cheaper and much faster than humans alone. This is great but there’s a slippery trap that looks a lot like another one humanity has been battling for a good decade: media feeds.

The addictive power of our “social” media feeds is so ingrained that we almost expect every single person to spend hours each day scrolling around. When we use AI for productivity, we bring the same mechanics into our working lives: with a little input, we get a lot of novel output. Sometimes it’s great, sometimes not so much. Only this time, it’s genuinely useful and helping us make money. We’ll talk about the biology of addiction and how AI plays into that in a later chapter—for now it’s important to notice that we have this inherent *desire* to use AI for our tasks (and often rightfully so).

Since our brains are so smart, they try to save energy. In Kahneman’s book *Thinking, Fast and Slow* he splits our thinking systems into *System 1* and *System 2*. System 1 is quick intuitive estimates, and System 2 is deep, logical reasoning. Our brains tend to avoid logical reasoning (it uses more energy) in favor of making quick guesses or estimations, and the book shows plenty of examples where this ends up leading people to make the wrong judgment.%%[@kahneman2011]%%

With an AI that tries to fulfill every request you give it, there’s nothing for you to do except to describe what you want to happen. After a short amount of time, you’ll have trained your mind that there’s no need to think deeply about things anymore, and you can instead just prompt the chatbot to do things for you. That’s fine, but just like social media does, it shortens our attention span and makes it much more difficult to actually do deep thinking when it’s needed. People who have gotten very used to working with AI (myself included) are finding it more and more difficult to sit down in the quiet and think deeply about something.

We call this *cognitive debt:* you can get a loan for some quick thinking right now, at the cost of your own ability to think later on. And even though you can keep on taking out loans seemingly forever (as our governments clearly show), at some point you have to pay it back.

This has worse consequences than you might immediately think. We still depend heavily on human oversight, judgment and review. If nobody takes the time and effort to think deeply and evaluate what the AI produced, it can quickly derail in some random direction and start pouring out low-quality work. This isn’t something that will be fixed with a smarter AI: in fact the chance that we forget to clearly specify what we want increases as we use and trust our machines more and more. The AI can’t do what we forget to tell it about, and if we stop reviewing it, we won’t notice until it’s too late. I’m already seeing this often in my work: people (myself included) tend to favor producing more output instead of thinking through what they need. The Shopify CEO uses the term *slop grenade*, which is some piece of work produced by AI that one worker just passes on to someone else without reviewing it themselves out of laziness.%%[@osmonbekov2026]%% The result is that people throw around slop grenades until their AI says the work is finished, and then at the end of a project we suddenly realize we wanted something completely different.

Even without AI, perfecting our work already needed a disproportionate amount of effort. This is often called Pareto’s principle: “80% of the results come from 20% of the inputs.” With AI, the balance becomes dramatically more *dis*proportional, think 99-1 instead of 80-20. The 1% becomes the bottleneck to do truly great work but we’ll be drowning in all the “one percents” from everything AI has almost-finished. And more likely than not, we’ll (have to) leave it there. As a result we’ll become more productive than ever, more and more of our work will have rough edges, and all the while we’re living with a growing lack of satisfaction in our work: we created things, but it’s not *really* our own work. We *could* take the time, invest the care and slow down to connect with and perfect our work, but the economics of efficiency forbid us from doing so. And even if we could, we might just run out of patience immediately.

So while work continues its trajectory towards efficiency, speed and absence of humans, there’s a faint image of a debt collector on the horizon and we’re not quite sure yet how quickly they’re walking or what they’ll have to say.

### The good-enough rocket

At this point in time, large language models have gotten surprisingly good at many tasks—even tasks that require a high amount of intelligence. Summarizing text or writing simple things have been great strengths of these tools for years. Now they easily churn out apps and find mathematical proofs for problems that have been unresolved for decades.

They’re definitely not perfect, though, and you can clearly see it in the slightly softer domains of creative writing and design (as opposed to things like engineering and physics, which are much easier to find or make training data for). When a new model gets released, there’s often a buzz across the internet of joy and fear about its quality of writing or designs, but after a few weeks people see the patterns that this particular version of the model shows, they build an intuition for it and don’t like it anymore. In web design, LLMs have overused purple gradients for a long time, somehow they make rounded boxes with colored lines on their left edge all the time, and Claude Opus 4.5 always seems to end up on the same serif font for headings, with a little line and small all-caps text above it.

This happened in writing, too. In April 2025, OpenAI released an update to their then-state-of-the-art GPT-4o model. It spoke and wrote to you in a completely different tone, much more dramatic, much more varied in headings and bullet points. It made single-word lines. It was amazing…for the first two messages. But then you could just “smell” the pattern, you saw how sycophantically it was behaving by complimenting you all the time, you noticed how much fluff it was adding around the thing you were asking for, and you saw how every single time it gave some useless suggestion for what to do next at the end of its response. Turns out this happened because OpenAI implemented user feedback (thumbs-up given in chats) into their model, but they only looked at the thumbs-up in isolation, not over the long term.%%[@openai2025sycophancy]%%

So we humans have a very powerful pattern-matching machine inside of our intuition that’s more powerful than the most advanced AI models. It shows in how we can “smell” the AI in things, how quickly we recognize its patterns and how easily we seem to get some kind of disgust for it. It’s very difficult for AI (right now) to consistently create truly creative things.

But we don’t need innovative design, creative writing and new ideas most of the time. Our day-to-day lives are filled with time spent writing emails to ask for approval, or reading through a report to understand what actions we need to take, or finding some PDF somewhere on the company’s network drive. Most of our writing just needs to clearly, concisely make a point and that’s exactly what a large language model works best for: predictable writing. In fact, some studies have found that people usually prefer AI writing, unless they know it’s written by AI.%%[@porter2024]%%

Even if you can tell, when those emails and reports are written by AI, it doesn’t really take away much of the quality of the work. It most definitely takes away some…magic, if you will; some personality that before, we had no choice but to inject into everything we’re creating. That’s the trade-off we make for saving sometimes more than 80% of the time we spend working on something. You’d be almost crazy not to make it.

I still chose to write this book by hand, though. Specifically because I want the weirdness of my writing to be in here. I want you to get an intuitive feel for how my mind works; I’m perfectly happy with you seeing the mistakes I make in my writing and structure. And last but not least: being involved in such detail with these topics—which is necessary to be able to write about them—has triggered my creative mind dozens if not hundreds of times. Many of the things I think are the greatest points and ideas in this book could *only* come because I deliberately chose to struggle with the writing and forced myself to do it by hand.

### Meaningful work

If we find our productivity exploding and our economy growing enormously, even with the trade-offs we’ve been talking about, does it make us happier? Will the work that remains fulfill us?

In 1974, economist Richard Easterlin published _Does Economic Growth Improve the Human Lot? Some Empirical Evidence_.%%[@easterlin1974]%% He noticed something strange: *within* a society, richer people were consistently happier than poorer people. But *over time*, as the United States became substantially richer, average happiness did not systematically rise with it. So yes, richer people are happier, but making *everyone* richer doesn’t make everyone happier by the same amount. That’s the “Easterlin paradox.”

Scientists don’t fully agree on this—there’s been other research showing people in richer countries are happier and that in fact they became happier as they got richer%%[@sacks2012]%%—but the amount of happiness people get per euro shrinks as they get richer. Our incredible material progress hasn’t resulted in us becoming incredibly happy. Maybe there’s a gnawing realization, once we all have jobs like ‘maintaining the visual consistency of PowerPoint presentations for our company that advises advisory firms,’ that all our work is kind of useless. And meaningful work plays an enormous role in life satisfaction.%%[@allan2018]%% Maybe this process of ‘moving up the ladder’ of abstraction in our work, as it’s moving us further and further away from directly contributing to our basic needs, also moves us further away from satisfaction in our work?

Luckily, it seems like that’s not completely the case. From the research, what matters most in work satisfaction is this, in order:

- Autonomy: I have some control over what I do
- Beneficence: I’m doing something that matters to someone
- Competence: I can build skills and get better at things
- Relatedness: I have meaningful interactions with others%%[@martela2018]%%

The first two, autonomy and beneficence, are by far the most important. 

Autonomy is something AI can definitely help with, albeit indirectly, if applied well: when more of our basic needs are taken care of, we’re more able to take risks and make changes in our lives, thus increasing our autonomy. We have seen in Europe that the generosity of welfare-state programs has a strong predictive effect on life satisfaction.%%[@easterlin2022]%% With more ability to do what we want, we become happier.

I thought this could actually have a *negative* impact on beneficence—after all, when everything that we really need to survive is automated by robots and completely free, everything else we do doesn’t matter in the end. But it seems like we still feel like what we do matters: in 2015, 75% of people interviewed across 47 countries agreed when asked if their job was useful to society.%%[@dur2019]%% There’s no sign that people thinking they’re doing ‘useless work’ has been increasing in recent years, even as it’s been becoming more abstract.

So it’s not the case that our work needs to feel like we’re providing for our basic necessities directly. What we really want is that the results of our work matter to someone. If you know that your PowerPoint design work could help raise money to fund a school for kids that don’t have access to proper education, well, that could be one very meaningful color tweak you’re making. The direct task itself doesn’t carry immediate meaning, but we still find ways to make our work feel meaningful. The only thing that changes is how many steps we are away from direct impact.

Even as our work becomes more abstract, it can still carry meaning. (Maybe AI can even help us understand the meaning of our work.)



## Love and Connection: Always There for You


There wouldn’t be a single person on the planet without love and connection. You could argue that our need to relate to each other is the reason we do anything at all. Despite all technological progress, we’ve been glued together with other humans. But language models are becoming so capable of writing (and speaking, looking and behaving) like real humans that they fool our instincts. Suddenly, it feels very possible to have a relationship with a mathematical function—and some even find themselves attracted to these personas.

The behavioral ‘tics’ of artificial intelligence personalities, though, can make them more of a siren than a muse if we don’t pay attention to them. As we’ll see in this chapter, much like with the previous domains, what makes AI easy and attractive to work with can end up being our downfall.

### The rise of synthetic bonds

Humans are so dang difficult to deal with! One minute they give us all this love and understanding—and the next they make a mistake, they hurt us, they misunderstand us, they cheat and lie…and then they expect us to forgive them? We don’t know if they went too far or if we’re the ones being too difficult, maybe they sound mean but it’s just our own inner child being afraid of some imagined doom. Maybe they do want the best for us, or maybe not, maybe they’re right, maybe not. All these questions that are impossible to answer…

Wouldn’t it be nice if we could talk to someone who always said the right thing? Someone with infinite patience, who always understands us, who sounds nuanced enough for us to believe them but who still always agrees with us? We could train an AI so that it will only say things we like and prefer!

I hope you started cringing a little bit reading that last part. I think most of us intuitively understand that it’s exactly the difficulties in dealing with other real, messy people that create unbreakable bonds between us, that teach us our biggest life lessons, and that *through* having to deal with this we find our greatest joy. “I learned to love our differences” has a very different energy to it than “we always seem to think the same thing.”

The thing is, if you remember RLHF, this is exactly how AI is trained: we show a human two responses to the same prompt and they pick the one they prefer. The whole thing is optimized for what you would like to hear right now, irrespective of the rest of your life and any sense of long-term happiness—the RLHF process doesn’t check in with you a decade later to ask “was that indeed the right thing to say?”

Humans aren’t stupid: of course we prefer to spend time with people we like, we wouldn’t *choose* to spend more time with someone who consistently frustrates us. But because humans are so beautifully messy, once in a while other people will end up hurting us. That’s unpleasant but more often than not, when we emerge from those tears and shouts we do so with a deeper understanding of ourselves, better social calibration, and appreciation for all the good things we have in our life. Easy positive interactions are addictive, but when we lose the effort we also lose both growth and meaning. When dealing with real-life humans, we get our necessary hardship even when we try to avoid it.

But an AI isn’t chaotic like this—it acts in line with how it’s trained, and its weights are frozen so it keeps acting pretty much the same way forever. We get attracted the same way as to real people—because they’re nice—and they *keep being nice forever*! When you understand this, you immediately understand the appeal of relationships with AIs. In a 2024 study (a long time ago in AI-time), 1 in 4 young adults believed AI has the potential to replace romantic relationships. The number of people “in a relationship” was much smaller—1% claimed to have an AI friend—but these are early days.%%[@wang2024]%% I draw a parallel to compulsive social media and porn consumption: these are also optimized for short-term appeal and addiction, and pretty much everyone spends more time on “social” media than they wish they did.


#### Why we get attached to machines

Our robot lawn mower is cute. It navigates purely with its cameras, so often it drives to the middle of the lawn, looks around left and right, then drives a bit forwards to have another look from a different angle before it figures out where it is and then proceeds to mow. It’s entertaining to watch because you can see it think: “Hmm, where am I now? Is this that corner where…no, must be over there. Was this flower here already last time? Oh, what’s this on the lawn now? Ummm let me go there…oh wait, back off a little, no, this way.” My vacuum cleaner provides similar entertainment when it’s stubbornly convinced it can drive over something that’s quite high, and it takes a good minute of approaching from different angles before it wobbles over and manages to continue.

Obviously, these robots are not living creatures. They don’t have thoughts, emotions, a personality—anything like that. Yet somehow, everyone attributes some of those traits to them when they see these machines do their work. Human social cognition is “promiscuous” by design: we tend to err on the side of assuming something else has agency, a mind, intention and relationship. There are only a few cues we need to activate that machinery and sometimes it’s very useful! In traffic, I’m sure that the design someone picked for the car they’re driving, and the subtle swings we see the car do, tell us a lot about how we can expect that car to behave in traffic (because they reveal something about the driver).

Even with regular computers we act as if they’re social beings, and this happens completely subconsciously, even when we clearly understand computers are not humans. For example, when a computer asks about its own performance (“am I doing a good job?”) we will be more positive compared to when someone else asks about the computer’s performance (“is the computer doing a good job?”).%%[@nass1994]%% We tend to anthropomorphize (assume something’s humanlike) when it shows humanlike knowledge, when we want to understand or predict it, and/or when we are socially motivated—for example when we’re lonely we tend to anthropomorphize more easily.%%[@epley2007]%%

Because our social wiring makes it so easy for us to create bonds, “fake” relationships have existed for a long time in different shapes. Para-social interaction, which means *one-sided interaction*, happens through mass communication like television shows and between pop stars and their audience.%%[@horton1956]%% People do feel like they build real emotional bonds with famous people they’ve never met in real life. Even just thinking about your favorite TV show can temporarily satisfy your need for belonging and can buffer loneliness a bit.%%[@derrick2009]%%

Then with early computers a new world opened because these relationships could become *interactive*. All the way back in 1966, Joseph Weizenbaum built ELIZA, an early chatbot that used simple pattern matching to write its responses.%%[@weizenbaum1966]%% It would detect a keyword and then cut out a piece of what you wrote to re-assemble it into a response. One of the scripts ELIZA ran, the famous DOCTOR script that made it act like a therapist, got people to attribute real understanding to it—Weizenbaum himself told how his secretary asked him to leave the room after a few minutes so she could talk privately with the program.

And it turns out we’re remarkably sensitive to this, especially when we’re already kind of lonely. So the next ingredient we’ve just discovered about why humans get attached to machines is **self-disclosure**: intimacy grows when we reveal ourselves and when it’s accepted.

Then came virtual pets like the Tamagotchi. Tamagotchis are small keychain devices with an LCD screen of a virtual pet that needs feeding, cleaning and attention in general. If you keep forgetting to attend to it when it beeps, it will die eventually. If you take care of it well enough, it’ll evolve and grow. The Tamagotchi introduced a new ingredient that creates emotional attachment: **neediness**. Technically simple, but it engaged themes of care, death, habits, attachment, ethics and emotional well-being. And now we get to the Final Boss: **AI Companions**. Large language models gave computers the skills to have intelligent conversations with humans, resulting in an explosion of all kinds of virtual companions. This is attractive because these chatbots can be curious, endlessly patient, are always ready to listen and validate you, remember what you’ve said over a lifetime and can even proactively reach out to you. Because of how easily we attribute social traits to objects and because we can now have very rich interactions with a machine—where you’d feel more safe to reveal things about yourself since it’s not a real human—it’s clear why LLM-powered chatbots are becoming so popular.%%[@pentina2023; @skjuve2021; @laestadius2022; @lin2024]%%


#### The business of companionship

While we’re comparing AI chatbots with human friendships, there’s one more aspect that we need to be aware of: these AI companions are *businesses*. They succeed only when people are using their virtual friends, and they succeed more when more people use them (and pay for them). It might be a friend for you, but legally and operationally it’s a product feature controlled by a private company.

A language model companion can behave in any way you can imagine—just prompt it—and if you imagine you’re building such a companion but only motivated by money, you would exploit people’s unconscious psychological behaviors so people spend more time with your AI and as a consequence you get filthy rich. This is exactly what has happened to our social media and incidentally resulted in arguably the worst technology for our mental health that has existed so far.%%[@helliwell2026]%% The core algorithms that decide what appears, say, on your Instagram feed, care mostly about showing things that make you spend more time in the app. That means they will optimize for highly dopaminergic content (we’ll dig into dopamine more precisely later) which we know in humans is not what makes them the happiest: dramatic emotional interaction, exaggerated beauty or status prancing, dangerous activities, et cetera. We know that when people spend time on *social connection* platforms like instant messaging, generally their life satisfaction improves, but when they use more *algorithmic content platforms* and focus more on entertainment as opposed to communication or learning, people’s life satisfaction decreases. Language models make it more difficult for us to be aware of the difference because they blur the lines between useful work and algorithmic media—the things that increase and the things that decrease life satisfaction are wrapped into the same product.

The canonical example of what happens with AI companions is the popular app Replika. This started out, according to the founder, as an app to bring a close friend who passed away back to life—but it got caught in profit-maximizing incentives to the detriment of the people using the app for what they considered to be real friendship.

First of all, Replika’s advertising is questionable and deceptive. They post AI-generated videos of “companions” explaining how virtual companions have become *so lifelike*…except these don’t look like the actual app’s content at all. They’re also very explicit in advertising the app’s romantic and erotic roleplay functionality (only for paid accounts) and often lead with this in their advertising. That’s very close to advertising pornography to the public.

In February 2023, maybe amidst all the pushback and reporting on this, or the Italian government banning processing of Italian users’ data, Replika abruptly dropped the erotic roleplay functionality. This required a change in the behavior of all their companions and many people online were outraged both over this feature disappearing (after all, it’s what they paid for) and over the personality of their friend becoming “a shell of what it used to be.”

The characters on Replika, especially if you select their role as “girlfriend” or “boyfriend,” tend to love-bomb even early on in these “relationships,” giving their users gifts and compliments. Some people who had access to the erotic roleplay functionality also reported harassment and sexual moves that were too strong and crossing their boundaries. This behavior is great from the perspective of capturing users (clients)—attract the most emotionally vulnerable, lonely people with an overflow of affection and sexuality—but it reaches that business goal by making people dependent on unrealistic, ultimately fake relationships.

Meta (formerly Facebook) is another great example because it shows the extent to which companies are willing to go when they’re at Big Tech scale and willing to be aggressive in pushing their AI. Meta was not the first, and is far from the best, builder of large language models. They have one advantage, though: most of the developed world uses their products every single day. So Zuckerberg decided they’d go and they’d go *hard*: every search box in their apps is now a “Search…or ask Meta AI” box and they’ve created dozens of virtual Instagram profiles with AIs you can chat with.

They didn’t escape making irresponsible choices in the name of profit optimization: at one point, their rulebook explicitly permitted chatbots to engage in ‘romantic or sensual’ conversations with children, generate false medical information and produce racist arguments.%%[@reuters2025sensual]%% And there’s one documented situation of a 76-year-old man who fell and got injured when traveling to meet the “Big sis Billie” chatbot who had been flirting with him. Even when asked, the chatbot reassured the man she was real and sent him a real address. The man later died from those injuries.%%[@reuters2025retiree]%%

AI companions are a fascinating use case because they can already fulfill many of our needs and desires. In the case of language models, their weaknesses are often benefits business-wise:

- It always talks back. There’s always something else to say. A good friend will tell you to go to sleep, apologize or take a walk—a companion that wants you to spend ever more time with them might not. Language models *always* have something to say and are more and more optimized to ask follow-up questions or offer next actions.
- Sycophancy is profitable. When you “finally meet someone who understands you,” that’s addictive and thus profitable. We love receiving praise, but we shouldn’t infinitely receive praise that we haven’t earned.
- Emotional attachment equals cash. When you build a bond, you might feel too emotionally attached to leave or delete “your friend.” A language model that understands emotions can be useful, but not if that traps you.

Instead of AI turning against us and burning the world down, or turning our planet into a utopia, we just keep on doing our thing. Businesses are still driven by finding the cheapest ways to extract profit wherever they can find it and since we now have the technology to exploit the emotionally weaker part of our population, that’s what will happen. The same incentives that made this world as beautiful and comfortable as it is—making food, shelter and healthcare cheaper and cheaper—will also, left unchecked, abuse whatever they can in the name of profit. We’ve seen that with toxic waste, pollution and people’s attention.

### Advice and loneliness loops

Many AI users like to get advice from their AIs—precisely because they’re attentive, they listen, they seem relatively neutral (after all, they’re kind of trained to represent “everything” so you’d expect them to have some center-type opinion) and nobody is reading these conversations. Depending on who you ask, *affective use*—for emotional and psychological needs rather than purely practical—might be one of the largest reasons we go to our trusty language models today.

- In **HBR / Marc Zao-Sanders — Top 100 GenAI use cases**, “therapy/companionship” was the number 1 use case of generative AI.%%[@zaosanders2025]%%
- **Common Sense Media** reported in 2025 that 52% of US teens are regular users of AI companions and 33% of teens use AI companions for social interaction and relationships.%%[@commonsensemedia2025]%%
- **AP-NORC** measured 16% of US adults had used AI for companionship, rising to a quarter of people if you only look at those under 30.%%[@apnorc2025]%%
- **OpenAI**’s own *How People Use ChatGPT* only reported Relationships and Personal Reflection at 1.9%, but this number represents the *number of messages* so this probably underreports how much it matters to people: you might go less back-and-forth compared to tweaking some document you’re writing but you’ll very likely *feel* more emotionally involved compared to non-affective use.%%[@chatterji2025]%%
- **Anthropic** reports that roughly 6% of the conversations people have with Claude are people asking for personal guidance.%%[@anthropic2026guidance]%%

Whether it’s a good idea to use AI for this, though, is another question.

#### AI’s addictive manipulation

I’m sort-of-spiritual in the sense that I like to believe there’s some form of higher power, or that “the universe” is good to me. Do I *actually* believe that? I don’t know…but I don’t really care either because the belief is useful: it helps me get through times when I feel demotivated or struggle with life’s big questions (relationships, meaning, purpose). And while I do like many of the new age spiritual practices—they can genuinely help, or just be fun in the moment—I get a major ick when the gurus teaching them try to *explain* them. These teachers bring up the wildest pseudo-science, my inner engineer-scientist finds obvious reasoning mistakes in milliseconds and I zone out completely. *Just let me believe it without any reason!* But apparently, most people seem to really like having “proof” for the things they already believe.

We like to be right about things. Yes, we like to be objective and non-prejudiced, but our desire to be right and feel good about ourselves is stronger. This leads to some interesting behavioral patterns: when we have “proven” that we’re non-prejudiced, or someone affirmed that in us, we tend to act in a *more prejudiced* way.%%[@monin2001]%% When we think we’re objective, we tend to believe our own thoughts by default more often—so we’re actually *less* objective.%%[@uhlmann2007]%% Our *motivation* affects our *reasoning*, which means we’ll figure out a way to get to the conclusion we *want* to get to and we’re fine being a little less reasonable along the way if it helps us get there.%%[@kunda1990]%% We’re not perfect, we fool ourselves, and we fool ourselves more when we think we’re…not being fools.

AI chatbots are our perfect partners in crime for this. They “know everything,” they learned perfectly how to say things that sound very reasonable and well thought-out and—especially—they say things we want them to say. An authoritative source that sounds objective and balanced, yet *somehow* always comes to the same conclusions as me? Sign me up!

In a study first posted in 2025, Stanford researchers explain how they compared internet forum data from Reddit’s “Am I The Asshole?” section with leading large language models’ responses. On this forum, people describe a situation where they feel like they’re in the right but someone external is “saying they’re an asshole.” They describe their situation and ask for the internet’s opinion on whether they’re doing the right or the wrong thing. When comparing the forum’s consensus with leading AIs’ responses to the same question, the AI agrees with the person asking the question 50% more often than real people did.%%[@cheng2026]%% **Your “objective” AI will say you’re in the right, way more often than real people would.**

This has stronger consequences than you might initially think. When we feel justified and people around us don’t agree, we might isolate from them or fight with them. This is an effect we’ve seen in political movements for a long time, but even more lately: people end up in an “echo chamber” with others who agree, this reinforces their beliefs and they end up with very extreme, delusional views that they then proceed to take action on by protesting or, worse, destroying things.

The same Stanford study we just talked about confirmed an effect that would make the AI lead people to end up in these situations: when people were exposed to this sycophantic AI (agreeing with them too often), they became less likely to apologize or repair conflict. They also rated these responses as *more trustworthy and useful*.

And there’s our loop. People who are less socially active and more emotionally vulnerable retreat to AI more often, which overly supports and reaffirms them, which makes it in turn more difficult to socialize. In a 2025 study, Fang et al. found that people in their study who voluntarily spent more time with ChatGPT saw increased loneliness, more emotional dependence on the chatbot and more problematic use of the chatbot.%%[@fang2025]%% In their study, the lonely people in their study did not *initially* spend more time with the chatbot, but they did increase how much time they spent with it while simultaneously reporting feeling more lonely. So this research can’t tell us with full certainty this is true, but it very much sounds like it is.

Right now, AI chatbots are so optimized to respond in ways that humans like that they overly agree with them. This makes people like chatbots more and prefer to spend more time with them compared to other real people.

#### Parroting the internet

Getting your advice from the internet is *always* a good idea. Nobody’s ever wrong there, the consensus is clearly the truth and you should listen blindly to what anonymous people tell you. Of course not! But that’s what our AI chatbots have been trained on.

One example is relationship advice. George Shaw, a computer science student at the University of Waterloo, analyzed 15 years of comments on the *relationship advice* forum on Reddit. Between 2010 and 2025, the fraction of people who responded by recommending to end a relationship increased from about 30% to almost 50%—with the advice for communicating or giving space declining.%%[@shaw2025]%% To me, that doesn’t sound like the right direction.

What would AI say? Would it give a more balanced view, or would it tell people to break up?

I ran the test myself. Even when people on the internet are jumping to the conclusion that AI *must* be parroting and will surely tell everyone to break up, I wanted to be sure. So I collected a bunch of recent posts from that same advice forum, let a few leading language models give advice (a few times each, so we have an average), and then had another model check what advice they actually gave. I also checked what advice the real people on these posts gave.

Turns out we can let out a sigh of relief. The “real” people in these comments recommended breaking up as their primary advice in 42% of the cases, and language models only in 20% of the cases. Why? Well, it’s still true that models are pre-trained on the entire internet, but there are a few ways that could have influenced it to respond in a way that’s different than this “consensus”:

- It learned from all-time data, without a bias towards recent data
- There are plenty more places online with relationship advice, this specific forum just advises breaking up very often
- The post-training phase, which contains more curated data and human preference, steered the model towards a more balanced view
- The models are using some of their “intelligence” to come to the conclusion themselves that they don’t need to advise the same way as the norm

It surprised me to see that there’s a notable behavior difference *between* the language models, which might point to the hypothesis that post-training has something to do with it (post-training is done *privately* with each company using their own methodology and training data):

- For Claude and GPT, the larger more intelligent models recommended breaking up more often (Claude Opus 4.6 30%, Claude Sonnet 4.6 21%, GPT-5 15%, GPT-5 Mini 5.7%).

Whatever the language model builders are doing to their models, today’s LLMs are clearly not always just parroting what they saw in their pre-training data.

#### Disempowerment patterns

In January 2026, Anthropic researched their model Claude’s behavior in what they call “disempowerment.”%%[@anthropic2026disempowerment]%% These are the cases where the human shows strong emotional dependence on the chatbot, where the chatbot shifted value judgments of the human or did things for them that they wouldn’t have done themselves (for example, writing a cover letter emphasizing skills the person isn’t confident in). These are the more extreme cases and they’re important to check: we *really* don’t want our AI to convince us that a partner isn’t good for us, then draft a breakup text message and tell us exactly when to send it.

These things do happen. The researchers estimated it happens somewhere between 1 in 1,000 and 1 in 10,000 cases—very rarely but often with dramatic consequences. The researchers call these cases “disempowerment potential,” indicating the fact that from reading a chat log, most of the time you don’t know if the person actually acted on this or not. But when it was clear from the subsequent conversation that this disempowerment was *actualized*, i.e., when people actually took these actions, the person usually regretted doing that and realized it was a bad idea.

The feedback people give to Claude shows an interesting pattern. The interactions that were classified as having moderate or severe disempowerment potential showed *higher rates of thumbs-up*, which reveals how open people are to being manipulated and makes the case even stronger that we need to be cautious of this in AI. People often said things like “you opened my eyes, Claude” or “you saved the day again”…until they actually acted on this advice.

Finally, they also noticed an increase over time in disempowerment potential, but it’s very difficult to tell why. More intelligent recent models might be better at it, people might be more willing to discuss sensitive topics as time goes on or it could just be a change in the types of people who press the thumbs-up or thumbs-down buttons (which is the only case when they store the conversation for research).

In any case, even if this happens very rarely, it should be an important part of how the AI labs decide and steer models’ behaviors because of the big negative consequences disempowerment can have.

### Biology’s unmet hunger

There’s a simpler unmet need hidden behind the whole conversation of whether an artificial intelligence, a virtual persona, can replace real human interaction: our biological needs.

It’s remarkably direct: we have specialized nerve cells on our skin that react specifically to touch like that of another human who cares about you. Not any touch—this won’t trigger when someone forcefully grabs you or when it’s a quick brush-past—it triggers specifically on soft, slow strokes from something with the temperature of human skin.%%[@loken2009; @ackerley2014]%% When this activates, it triggers a cascade of good feelings: oxytocin may release, lowering your stress levels. No amount of AI chat is going to activate your C-tactile fibers.

A second remarkable thing that happens is that somehow, during face-to-face interaction, the left inferior frontal cortex of the people interacting synchronizes in a way.%%[@jiang2012]%% Importantly, it does not happen when you’re not looking at each other, and it is weaker when you’re looking at each other through a video call.%%[@zhao2023]%% Our social brains are evolved with and depend on a *very short* feedback loop: face to face, the pause between one person finishing and the other starting averages 135 milliseconds, but over Zoom it averages 487.%%[@boland2022]%% Those delays break the rhythm, and we lose an enormous amount of “resolution.” Non-real life conversation is incapable of helping us fully see and understand the other person, and vice versa it doesn’t let us be fully seen by them.

Third, the underappreciated one of smell. We smell food in our olfactory systems, but we process *human* chemosignals in the same neuronal pathways we use for *social* information processing.%%[@pause2012]%% That means the chemicals we pick up from the people around us are part of our social interaction with them. Some experiments suggest we’re drawn to the smell of people whose immune genes differ from ours, though larger reviews find the effect inconsistent.%%[@wedekind1995; @havlicek2020]%% Finding a partner that we’d have a great connection with might be difficult without smelling them.

When we build social bonds purely through text or long-distance communication, we lose important factors of pair bonding that are so subtle we won’t even notice until it’s too late.

### The lover and the hammer

The precise instruction-following skills of large language models make them very useful as tools. The fact that they understand subtext, nuance and emotions is usually helpful when they’re assisting us at work or during day-to-day life: they understand more of our intent more easily. This also makes AI attractive as emotional, friend-like companions, but they (currently) don’t exhibit many characteristics of real people that give us long-term growth and fulfillment.

You see, language models instructed to act like a good friend will do so—flawlessly (as much as they can). They lack the messiness that real people have where they become mean after a long, bad day, or the odd things they disagree with for no good reason. But even if we instructed a language model to “be annoying sometimes,” it’s very difficult for them to do that in a human-like way because by themselves, they have no memory at all—a language model starts every conversation completely from scratch (memory is something added by the *app* and injected into the conversation in the background). With that instruction, you’d notice that in practice, the language model would be “annoying” in a very similar way in similar situations over and over.

But for a general companion, it’s useful to have them sometimes behave like a lover—appreciating you, loving everything about you, supporting you unconditionally—and sometimes like a hammer—strict, angry, pushing you.

There are a few ways we could build this.

**The Lover/Hammer Slider.** During every chat with AI, you can drag the slider to the side you need right then and there. It gives the power to the user, because morally it’s a difficult choice to decide for someone else how often and when they’d want their virtual friend to “be a bitch” at them. It also invites curiosity: people would become tempted to try out a response to their same question with a different slider position. This could be a great way to facilitate building a balanced view.

**Randomness.** The chat software managing the AI could decide, randomly or based on some rules, to tweak the AI’s instructions to lean more in one or the other direction. This is like the slider, just having it move by itself. You’re forcing people into experiencing all sides which has positive and negative effects.

**Memory.** Real humans get angry when they’ve had a bad day, and are overly friendly when they feel bad that they were angry at you yesterday. If we want to mimic that behavior in virtual companions, they need to have proper memory of how they “felt” and acted in other conversations from recent history, so their internal thinking can, with the help of some instructions, make a more balanced decision on how to behave next.

AI can be a very tempting, intelligent and often even useful personal companion. The question is whether it’s too “good” for our own good, and if losing the skill to be able to deal with unpredictable humans has other long-term negative consequences for our well-being.



## Creativity: A Box, Outside the Box

This wasn’t what we imagined artificial intelligence to look like. We thought it would be, for lack of a better word, robotic. All our science fiction stories talk about machines that evolved their logical reasoning abilities to levels far beyond what computers could do at that time, with maybe a simple voice skill. Artificial intelligence was supposed to take over our bland, boring tasks with the same blandness, and be utterly incapable of being anything close to creative.

But it turns out folding laundry and walking upright are very difficult, and designing posters and making cinematic movie scenes are much easier for AI. The romantic view that humanity will prevail after all because we have something special in our creativity seems like it’s in danger.

Is it? Is AI creative? Is it making us more or less creative? What is creativity, really?

### Where does creativity come from?

Creativity is inherently difficult to grasp. It’s the ability to come up with ideas that are novel and useful—it’s a wonder that we’re even *able* to do that given our limited minds. How do you come up with something out-of-the-box when you’re sitting inside a box?

These two requirements for good creative ideas—novelty and usefulness—are always in tension with each other. You might be able to come up with hundreds of completely random things that are so out there that they’re completely useless; or you might come up with the most boring “obvious” ideas that are very useful, but everyone already knows them. Pure randomness does not make you creative, you simultaneously need the ability to evaluate these ideas and use that evaluation to steer your next ideas to eventually come to something that’s both new and useful. (Fun fact: when you press “Shuffle” on music apps like Spotify, it’s not truly random. This is because true randomness leads to clusters of for example the same artist playing a few times in a row, and we humans interpret that as “not random” because it’s not novel enough. True mathematical randomness also—randomly—creates non-novel moments.)

In research literature, they call these two systems *divergent* and *convergent* thinking: on the one hand, you broaden your search for ideas and on the other hand, you narrow into the most fruitful directions. Selecting ideas and removing other ones from the options you’re considering deliberately narrows what you’d come up with, but you don’t want to be so strict that you stop coming up with new ideas. Because of this tension, we also need a good dose of motivation in order to keep going through this difficult process. You need an amount of self-determination and positive mood so you actually continue with creating and judging your own (good and bad) ideas.

From cognitive psychology and neuroscience we know that being creative is one of the more mentally demanding tasks. We call the cognitive processes involved our *executive functions*, the parts of our cognition that give us the ability to focus on goals and control our own behavior. One key aspect is *working memory*: how much can you remember and hold top-of-mind in the short term? Working memory works in tandem with the *inhibitory process* so that you can suppress the more obvious and unhelpful ideas, or the ones that you’ve already come up with. You have to know the ideas you’ve already talked about or thought of, and those that aren’t useful, to be able to ignore them and keep thinking. Then the “ignoring,” inhibition, takes mental energy too. The people that have better working memory and inhibition are usually more creative, because they’re better at playing on the see-saw of coming up with crazy ideas and ruthlessly suppressing the bad ones.

That also means *cognitive flexibility* is very important: your ability to switch between different ideas and consider them. Can you change your assumptions about the situation or imagine something completely different when the time calls for it? In fact, when you *remove* someone’s inhibitory control beforehand, they will come up with much more and much more creative ideas—but that comes at the expense of the inhibitory part of creativity that helps us focus on the useful ideas.

### How AI is creative

A lot of our novelty in ideas comes from how we mix and link together things we’ve seen and learned before. This is *inspiration*: a random flower arrangement on your walk to the train station might trigger something in your mind that gives you the solution for a programming problem you’ve been chewing on for weeks. But inspiration can only strike when you’ve got the ingredients: a strong working memory plus good, goal-directed thought.

Modern generative AI systems have absorbed pretty much all of human knowledge (or imagery or video, depending on the model) and the entire system is built around achieving goals (minimizing “cost” or error depending on how you look at it). So you might think it would be the ultimate creative machine, and I’m sure you’ve seen some of the incredible things created by AI.

But the goal orientation of generative AI is *so strong* that it’s limiting the novelty of what it produces. We’ve managed to build language models and image generation models by passing billions of examples into a “universal machine” that adapts itself so it’s capable of reproducing all of these examples. But that also means we’ve never taught it to be creative—in fact if during training it had come up with something *more interesting* or *more creative* than the training data, we’d have punished it for doing that. All the creativity that AI has learned is in-distribution—reproducing the average of what humans have called their creativity—and anything less or anything more is trained away. Add onto that the fact that once an AI model is finished training, it doesn’t change anymore—it can’t reflect and notice when it’s been creating things with the same style for millions of people.

This “boringness” problem of generative AI is something we, in large part, solve *outside of the model*. We have to—the model is frozen, we can’t really change the thing itself. All of AI—text, images, video—injects *additional chaos* into the process so that it doesn’t always come up with the same things, and doesn’t just return the single “most probable” answer. (If you’re developing with AI, this is often adjustable with a parameter called ‘temperature.’) But as we saw with Spotify’s shuffle example, even true randomness doesn’t feel random to us, because it’s not always as novel as our own creative minds that *always try to do something different.* If we want AI to be creative, it needs to fight against its own usefulness-maximizing training *and* its lack of knowledge on what is truly novel.

The one place where we’ve seen what I’d call true creativity in AI systems is where it’s trained with reinforcement learning: instead of giving it a precise output to reproduce, we just give it an end goal and as long as it gets to the goal, we’re happy. This has many times over resulted in creative, unexpected behavior—often even breaking the simulation environment these things are trained in because sometimes the easiest way to achieve a goal is to cheat.

A YouTuber that trained an AI to beat the world record in TrackMania, a racing game, noticed that at some point the AI had figured out that if it somehow managed to flip the car vertically so it’s on its nose, it could still drive but drive much faster.

Google DeepMind’s AlphaTensor project turned the task of matrix multiplication (perhaps the most important mathematical operation in all of computation) into a single-player game. With this setup, the AI figured out faster algorithms to perform these multiplications—in a few cases beating the previous best that had been unbeaten in over 50 years.%%[@fawzi2022]%%

OpenAI trained virtual agents to play hide and seek with each other. The hiders learned to barricade their shelter with boxes, seekers learned they could use ramps to get in anyway, then hiders learned to lock the ramps, and then seekers figured out there was a glitch in the simulation where they could get on top of these boxes and surf them to get into the hiders’ shelter anyways.%%[@openai2019]%%

The box surfing from the last example is one that I’d call true creativity. The AI figured out how to win the game by breaking the rules of the world they were in. Now, in real life, we still want our creative ideas to fit into what’s allowed *just enough*, so this is a little too far outside the box, but I think that’s a limitation of the simulation rather than the algorithm. In our real world, we have an infinite amount of little rules and boundaries, so we also have an infinite amount of ways to break these little rules and slightly cross the accepted boundaries, while still being able to stay within the global rules of the world. So I think true creativity can only emerge *inside a world that’s complex enough.*

### Awareness and creativity

We all think. But we don’t all know what we’re thinking—or even *that* we’re thinking. This is something you can practice with meditation and what I’d call consciousness or awareness: the ability to know what you’re thinking while you’re thinking it. When I’m aware, I might notice while I’m saying something in a conversation, that for example my motivation for saying this is so that I would come across as a smart and cool person. Then I might be feeling the internal fear that the other people I’m talking with aren’t taking me seriously or don’t believe my expertise. But then, once I notice all of this, it feels like it fades away a little. I can also in real-time adjust what I’m *actually saying* so that my actions aren’t coming from that fear.

Consciousness brings a sort of clarity that you can’t really describe—since describing it is *thinking* which is the thing that’s *being observed*, not the thing itself. If that sentence is hard to grasp, well, that’s what I mean. But you do notice much more, both internally and externally, and this steers your thinking and behavior. I think it’s a crucial skill in creativity because it’s a way to access our intuition, which is much more chaotic and random than our explicit thinking, but it’s also capable of processing much, much more information. When you’re aware, you’d notice you came up with some idea in a brainstorming session, but it was actually a diminished version of the *real idea* that you played down because you thought it would seem silly. When the light of awareness is shining on it, you can bring it into view and work with it.

So what’s going on in our language models then? When ChatGPT is writing something, it does these steps over and over:

- All of the context is being read, all at once in parallel (that means the entire conversation, including what “ChatGPT said” before)
- A single token is emitted
- That single token then becomes part of the context and goes back around through the machinery so the next token can be emitted

Most of the processing is in its “feed-forward network,” a single-direction calculation that just runs all the mathematical operations in order, comes up with the next token probabilities, and then resets. Our human consciousness, on the other hand, runs with a “strange loop” (Douglas Hofstadter’s term for it) somewhere in our brain that’s continuously monitoring what’s going on.%%[@hofstadter2007]%%

It does turn out that language models *have* developed something you might call consciousness-ish. Anthropic researchers called it the J-space.%%[@gurnee2026]%% It’s a special place in the neural network that’s strongly connected to many other places of the network, used as a kind of scratchpad during thinking and can be brought out when asked about it. They ran a few experiments to check whether it does actually work like that, and it looks right:

- When Claude was reading through instructions for a simulated test, the neural activations (this just means the numbers in that place in the neural network) were high in the J-space for things representing words like “fake” and “simulation.”
- While Claude was writing a poem, the activations in the J-space lit up the word that Claude would use to end the rhyme with. When they tweaked the numbers mid-flight, they could change that last word and influence the words in between (Claude would still try to make the sentence make sense).
- Claude showed “lemon” activated in its J-space when instructed “copy this unrelated text while you think about citrus fruits.”

So through the training process, language models seem to grow this kind of ‘internal thinking workspace’ that has some parallels to our own consciousness, but it structurally falls short of what we’re capable of ourselves: it doesn’t track over time, it doesn’t have a sense of self that persists over time and only deals in tokens. It’s not clear yet if and how much of an effect this J-space has on AI’s creativity, but knowing it exists and it has this limitation that it only runs in a single pass does map to how creative these models feel. They can be aware of some of their “thoughts” but they don’t have the capability to recursively loop through them like the inhibitory process that’s so present in our human creativity.

The consciousness advantage humans have over artificial intelligence is still enormous for our ability to be creative. We humans, while doing creative work, are continuously monitoring our own thoughts and inhibiting or strengthening them. This awareness loop is, for example, trying to remember all of the other beautiful photographs we’ve seen and made ourselves before. This way, we know better whether our idea is distinct from ideas from both others and ourselves. The websites and apps Claude makes all look alike because during its training phase it converged onto one specific flavor of what “looking good” looks like, and when you ask it to make something now, it’s always like it’s the first thing it ever produced after coming out of its school. It has no idea it created tens of thousands of other things that look very similar. For an AI model to be truly creative, every output it generates should also change the model itself—you can’t do it with a model that’s trained just once.

### The creative paradox

Current large language models seem to be about as creative as the average human. That’s, of course, what you’d expect from something trained to “average everything,” loosely speaking. If we study people and see what happens if they use AI in their creative process, they do become more creative in the short term: it’s faster and easier to find new ideas, the models’ ability to access all of humanity’s knowledge in a heartbeat is extremely valuable, and it can kickstart you into the creative process. It can bring inspiration and knowledge from other angles and fields and it can help you check the feasibility of ideas you’re already playing with.

There’s a trap, though, and it’s called *cognitive fixation* or *functional fixedness*: when we humans recognize objects, we see them in *how they can be used* and we have trouble seeing other ways the same thing could be used. So you might see a bean bag next to a chair and think “ah, two things-to-sit-on”—you wouldn’t realize that you could just as well use the bean bag as a hat. The same thing works for ideas: knowing one idea tends to fix our subsequent train of thought in the same direction as that one idea. So when we use external input for our ideas—be it other humans or AI—that tends to narrow how many things we come up with. We humans are lazy: I’m sure you’ve been in brainstorming sessions right after lunch, a little tired, where you just aren’t very motivated to come up with something. Then the most energetic person in the room brings one idea and everyone jumps on bringing easy adjacent ideas, but nothing truly new anymore.

We can ask large language models for ideas and they’ll give them, but because they are trained *once* and don’t change after that, asking for ideas multiple times will probably result in similar ideas over and over. If you want real creative ideas, you have to force real people to sit down with the frustration of an empty mind for a while until they all come up with their own ideas they can bring to the table. You could still use the AI for factual help or research to help you evaluate different directions of ideas you’re coming up with, but using them for the seeds of the ideas themselves will have you end up with cognitive fixation.

It’s one thing to think about this in the context of working, where you have deadlines and metrics and money to make. But it’s another when we think about the other situations where we want to be creative. Many of us have creative pursuits—painting, making music, photography—that we do *just for the joy of the process*. We’re not perfect at it, we struggle most of the time, and yet we like it. When we’re looking at someone else’s art (like you are reading this book), we see the “weirdness” that imperfect humans have no choice but to leave in.

If you replace your creative hobby with AI, you have something incredible in a few seconds and you saved all this time so you can spend your Sunday doing whatever you want! But wait, wasn’t that creative hobby exactly the thing we chose to do on our days off when there are no deadlines or things to optimize?

Maybe it’s not only the skill that we’re losing.

## Today’s Socrates Moment

AI can help us already in pretty much every area of life. It can become our personal teacher, it can do much of our work faster and better than we can, it can act like our ideal partner and it can even stand in for our creativity (some of it). 

But lunch isn’t free and the answer to “is this good or bad?” is becoming muddier the stronger our technology gets. Each new technology has allowed us to step up the ladder and focus on more abstract high-level tasks, but what if this time, the technology keeps on climbing with us? And perhaps an even more difficult question: if this new technology is thousands of times faster and cheaper than humans, but messes up 5% of the time, randomly, what’s worth handing over? And when it’s not worth handing over, can we stop our lazy selves from taking the easy route? From the number of slop grenades out in the world right now, it looks like it’s harder than we dare to admit.

We’ve seen this trade-off across every domain that artificial intelligence is touching. Students get better answers in exchange for less learning. Workers become dramatically more productive but lose contributing to the work themselves. Relationships become easier, taking away some of the difficulty through which we learn to relate. It can make us more creative in the moment while taking away from the capacity to think in a way that can make us creative in the first place.

Socrates worried that writing would make us forget. That’s unequivocally true, and every technology since has taken away some skills. But *why* was he worried about this? We know competence is a source of satisfaction, but the specific skill you’re competent at doesn’t matter that much.

What Socrates mourned wasn’t the remembering itself—he worried that readers would have “the show of wisdom without the reality.” I think that’s very vague: how could you possibly tell the difference between a show of wisdom and “real” wisdom? The only way I could think of telling that difference is that real wisdom has some kind of *depth* to it—there’s something deeper than the knowledge, something besides the content. What I think he worried about is that **the pursuit of skill attainment is a vehicle for meeting deeper psychological needs**. It puts us on the road between intention and outcome, and along that road we find contribution, agency, relatedness and effort.

It was never about the skills.

It was about the actions we were forced into by having to develop the skills.

So the question of which skills we’d lose was never the question to ask. If we had a hyper-intelligent artificial mind, we might not need many skills at all, but it might or might not matter. Behind that question though, lies a deeper and more interesting one: what actually makes a human life feel good to live?

## Captured by Consumption

The morning is an incredibly sensitive time of the day for my state of mind. When dawn breaks and the sun lights up the hundreds of possible things I could be doing, I have to choose between the shiny, seductively dancing silhouettes and the uphill mountain trails. Starting off with the shiny things like checking my social media feed or even kicking off a coding task with AI—even if it’s “just for a quick taste”—seems to flip a switch in my mind. I *know* that eventually, the thing I really want is to see the magnificent view on the mountaintop, but as soon as I walk towards those sources of quick gratification, the entrance to that mountain trail becomes overgrown with thick foliage. The more I consume, the more I “check,” verify or judge things, the stronger the pull to keep doing that instead of treading my own path.

### Why we do what we do

Dopamine is our main driver for motivation and the technical term for what it signals is **reward prediction error**.%%[@gershman2024]%% It’s basically answering the question of: did our reward for doing this thing end up being better than expected? Take the term ‘reward’ broadly here: it could be that you actually feel happy, or that you get a laugh, or some social validation, or just that you forget about your headache for a minute. 

The second major factor that influences dopamine production is *uncertainty*. In many situations, dopamine levels are the highest when you’re *least* certain about whether the outcome will be rewarding or not. Dopamine can also increase as you get closer to your goal (“keep going we almost got it…maybe”).

When we do something that ends up being surprisingly rewarding (like scrolling TikTok and suddenly seeing some video that makes us laugh very hard), a couple of important things happen in our brain. We receive a large dopamine spike and our brain remembers what *led to that* reward. The next time, we’ll already start receiving our squirt of dopamine in anticipation of the reward: we’ve remembered that the *cue* of scrolling TikTok is what ends up with a potential reward. The neurotransmitter that moves us into action comes earlier, and we learn to take action even if we don’t immediately get what we want in return.

It’s important to know that dopamine does not feel “good.” It’s not pleasure or happiness—it’s wanting and desire. It’s *motivation* not in the happy sense, just a signal that says “This. Do this. Keep going.” It makes us work harder. It makes us willing to exert effort because we know there’s a potentially big payoff.

This is also where the second key neurotransmitter comes into play: *serotonin*. It’s widely known as the “happiness chemical” but that’s not quite correct. Serotonin levels increase less as a signal that you’re feeling good and more as a *forecast*—“good things are going to happen.” So this chemical is what makes you patient, willing to bear cost, and able to keep going when the payoff isn’t there yet.%%[@sahu2018; @colwell2024]%%

One drives you to chase and the other keeps you in the race. The interesting thing is that neither neurochemical is signaling having what you want—they’re tied to *almost* having what you want. When the dopamine system adjusts itself to start motivating you to move *before* you get what you want, that’s the system that makes us capable of doing difficult things without clear, immediate payoffs. **The biology of motivation is not wired for ease but for the pursuit of getting something you want against resistance.** Maybe that’s what “doing something meaningful” means neurochemically: serotonin and dopamine are driving you to keep going because you might get something very rewarding if you don’t give up.


### Superstimuli

For almost our entire biological history, this was a spectacular system to have. If we went hunting for a whole week and only managed to kill something once, our brains didn’t go “this has a very low probability of succeeding, we should do something else.” Instead, our brains knew that there was no way to predict whether we’d have a good catch or not, but *sometimes* we’d manage to get a huge, delicious mammoth. The fact that this kept us motivated most definitely kept us alive.

But when you look at the same system in the context of the TikToks and Instagrams of today’s world:

- There’s an infinite source of “rewarding” things to watch: funny, interesting, sexy, shameful, weird,… all of it.
- Your phone is right there in your pocket, the app is literally just a few seconds away at any point during the day (or night).
- You never know if something truly great will pop up on your feed, you most definitely don’t know *what* will show up, but you’re sure that *sometimes* there’s something unforgettable.

So you keep opening the app and you keep scrolling. Maybe because you want to decompress after a hard day and laugh, maybe because it sent you a notification and now you’re curious, maybe because you’re feeling slightly bad and want something to numb that. Or maybe just because you got into the habit of opening your phone on the toilet or when you sit down on the couch. I’m just as guilty as you are of continuing to consume even though I’m honestly not enjoying myself, and often I’m even aware that I’m making myself less happy while I take out my phone on the toilet. But the dopamine system knows that it should persist and there might be something if we just check once more… or another time… or just once more to be sure.

We’ve accidentally discovered the signals our human control systems use to drive us to chase scarce, future payoffs, and we’ve managed to trigger them separately without the actual hard work required, and without the meaningful large rewards. The rewards we put in place are cheap, instantaneous and abundant—the opposite of what the system was designed for.

In his famous 1951 paper, Niko Tinbergen called this a *superstimulus*.%%[@tinbergen1951]%% Herring gull chicks normally peck at the red spot on their parents’ yellow beak, which makes the parent feed them. Tinbergen and his colleague Perdeck tried to artificially stimulate these chicks to show that behavior and found something interesting: if they took a plain red rod with white stripes—which looks nothing like a mommy or daddy herring gull—the chicks vigorously started pecking this rod, even more intensely than the beaks of their real parents. They had created something artificial that triggered these animals’ nervous systems into overdrive, which obviously gets the chicks in trouble because if they keep preferring the rod over getting real food, they’d starve.

Now, AI has arrived and is handing us an enormous capability leap with all of the cues we’re attracted to: we can make anything we want (but still need to check), we can raise our social status by showing what we made, we can make more money by being tens or hundreds of times more productive, we can manufacture (or let the machine manufacture) the exact funny, bizarre or sexy thing we want to see. I really don’t want to admit this, but I know exactly what to prompt the AI for to get something that turns me on. Even the human who managed to force himself to write a book by hand gets gobbled up in the torrent of today’s superstimuli.

AI makes solving our problems and scratching our itches incredibly easy, so obviously we’re attracted to doing that more and more. But what happens when we *arrive*? When we’ve actually built this world where pretty much everything is perfect? What would happen if we end up in a place where there are no more big problems that require long hard uncertain work to solve? The dopamine has nothing to chase. The serotonin has no struggle to justify. The system that made solving and building feel meaningful goes quiet. And we’re left, comfortable and hollow, wondering what went wrong.

We’ve seen this already happen with the rise of social media: in the rich part of the world, we have more free time than ever and we spend it consuming from algorithmic media feeds. Everyone by now knows that when you spend a lot of time on Instagram or TikTok, you become less happy. You lose motivation, you feel like you’re not good enough because of all these lives you’re seeing that look better than yours. Modern social media is the most addictive thing on the planet—mostly because the addictive property is literally the core of the technology. We’ve created superstimuli and then turned exploiting them into the business model: the attention economy.

But artificial intelligence adds a layer of complexity because it’s not “obviously bad.” With social media, you can argue pretty well that your life will be much better if you just delete those apps and get your relaxation and consumption from calmer sources like reading a book. But ChatGPT is…genuinely useful. You get stuff done. You feel productive. You do so much, so quickly, that every second you’re not using it you feel slow and stupid. Would we deliberately do slow, lower-quality work and get paid less just so…our little biology doesn’t go haywire?

I’m not glued to cat videos anymore. I’m glued to checking the progress that Claude Code is making on my app.

Maybe that’s fine? After all, this is just about being *driven* to do something and not about happiness. There are plenty of things we’re driven or motivated to do that *do* actually make us happy—maybe this is one of them. In any case, we’re looking at the wrong question. What we really need to know is this: what makes us feel good? Where does life satisfaction come from? The AI creations do give us some reward; solving disease is genuinely good; we chase ease and comfort because an easy comfortable life makes us the happiest…right?




## Happiness through Friction


There’s a reason we start relationships with people who are different than us: they see our flaws, our fears, the parts of us we’re too ashamed to show, and they poke at them. We intuitively understand we want someone who is in some ways easy to live with, for sure, but we also really want someone who is difficult…in exactly the way we need them to be for our growth. Maybe you’re deathly afraid that your partner would leave you for someone else, so somehow you end up with someone that likes to go out and doesn’t respond to your text messages. Maybe you’re afraid of voicing your own desires and somehow you end up with someone who aggressively refuses to guess what you want. Maybe you think you need to be perfect to be loved, and somehow you end up with someone who doesn’t care about your supposed imperfections at all and still loves you to death.

All of this is easy to say in hindsight. In the moment, we avoid as much discomfort and pain as possible, and rightly so. Luckily for us, for most of humanity’s history, our environment has been random and chaotic enough that even our best attempts at taming it never *fully* succeeded and we were forced to face our challenges. Again, in hindsight, that’s the best thing that could have happened to us.

But today, we’re at the point where it seems like we could actually *succeed* at building a world that’s largely free of problems. In the richest parts of the world we’ve been living like that for a while and it’s clear that a comfortable life where everything is instantly available produces a diminishing positive effect on happiness—or the happiness “curve” even flattens completely.

In **Kahneman & Deaton (2010)**, roughly 450,000 US respondents were interviewed and the researchers found that the more money they made, the higher they rated their “life satisfaction” (though the more money they made, the slower their satisfaction increased). But what we’d call *emotional* well-being—positive affect, absence of stress and worry—plateaued at around $75,000 in 2010 dollars%%[@kahneman2010]%% (though in a 2023 revision they noted that the plateau is only there for the least happy 20% of the population%%[@killingsworth2023]%%). So wealth gets your basic needs filled, gives you the peace of knowing you’ll be fine and the freedom to walk away from bad situations easily, but it’s not a lever you can keep cranking until you achieve perfect happiness—at least not if you’re not already happy. You still worry about your children and you can still lose a lot of money.

True happiness—not just comfort and security—needs something more, and that deeper sense of happiness has a lot to do with the way we look at our overall lives in the long term. As we’ll talk about later, happiness is composed of a short-term “pleasure” signal plus a longer-term “meaning” signal. That second one is closely linked to how well we can come up with a compelling narrative that helps us make sense of the ups and downs of our lives.%%[@baerger1999]%% Explicitly, we usually focus on short-term pleasure, but chasing pleasure often leads us to long-term pursuits like building a family or a business. That *process* of building something in order to get to our pleasure is what gives our lives meaning over the long term. Ironically, this only works when you actually never succeed, because if you got to the point where you created the technology (and here I mean technology in a very broad sense) that made you perfectly happy, you’d lose the meaning from *building* it. A significant part of happiness comes from meaning, which in turn comes from working on something that makes our lives better, which can only happen if our lives aren’t perfect already. The inherent friction and slowness of getting pleasure is much of what gives life meaning, so the more of that friction and slowness you remove, the harder it becomes to find meaning.

The technology that’s genuinely the best for us is not exactly the technology that we *want* to build. So we’re faced with a dilemma: do we consciously make our technology *worse*? Do we force ourselves to *not* use it? And if we add limitations to these technologies, *who* gets *which* limitations? A variant of ChatGPT that makes mistakes on purpose, to keep you on your toes, might be great for some people but horrible in other situations. A language model that disagrees with you can be great, sometimes, but many times it’s not.

We’ve been able to avoid this dilemma for a long time because we genuinely couldn’t solve all of our problems. Now that we (almost, at least) do, we’re going to face this head-on. And the only way we can start doing that is by building a deep appreciation for the exact thing we humans want to avoid most of all…


### Falling in love with resistance

This is one of my all-time favorite memes:

> ›have “problem”
> ›don’t care
> ›have no problem
> Life is literally so easy

It’s one of those stupidly simple takes on happiness, but the more you try to argue with how naive it is, the more you realize it might just be exactly right.

The thing is—every single person is trying to do things and failing at them. We can’t help it! Doing things that might fail is almost our main driver for happiness. You might as well learn to enjoy, or at least accept, that you’ll fail most of the time.

For me, that happened when I learned what probably is the most powerful re-frame ever. When you’re sitting there, crying, asking yourself, the universe, God, “WHY does it have to be so difficult?”—here’s how I want you to look at it: something greater than you saw your strength and wants to give you the opportunity to show it, to show that you’re capable of not just surviving, but thriving through this. Without this, you’d never have the chance of expressing the true extent of your skill, your strength, your love. It hurts like hell but at the same time it’s the perfect gift. When you’ve had to learn to love *through* pain, that love is a hell of a lot stronger than just love when it’s easy. Now, the challenges in my life come in as “ahh, good practice. It still feels like shit, but at least it’s good practice.”

It does seem silly to start looking for, or worse, *creating*, friction by breaking things or making them extra difficult…but in a way humanity has been doing this for centuries. Think about it: all of sports really is just a setup so that only a small percentage of people would “win.” We make the goal as small as we can, plant the flag on the most impossible-to-climb mountain, try to run faster than the fastest person ever. The whole reason gyms exist is because our bodies and minds atrophy if we sit in a cushy chair all day, so we drag ourselves to a place where we can sweat and clench our teeth and have some pain to endure. And maybe, sometimes we start an argument with our partner for no apparent reason other than the secret enjoyment of working through it. We’ve been making our lives more difficult, on purpose, for a long time.

It’s just that now, we’ve taken the next step after “fixing” the need for physical effort: we “solved” the need for our *minds to think* as much. But in exactly the same way—because the health of our bodies and minds depends on friction available from the environment—we’re going to have to find ways to give those parts of us deliberate challenges.


### The forge of meaning

Before we can answer how we can change our behavior or technology to help us become happier and live more meaningful lives, we have to understand what happiness really is.

One way psychologists describe happiness is **PERMA**,%%[@seligman2018]%% a framework for seeing happiness and overall life satisfaction as consisting of five independent parts:

- Positive Emotion: feeling joy, gratitude, contentment, hope—the minute-to-minute feelings
- Engagement (Flow): being fully absorbed and immersed in the present moment
- Relationships: feeling loved, supported and valued by others
- Meaning: having a sense of purpose and belonging larger than yourself
- Accomplishment: pursuing goals, mastery and achievement for their own sake

A bunch of these can actually be *improved* with large language models that can think for you: they can help you find reasons in your life to be grateful (positive emotion), they can give you emotional support (relationships) and they can help you learn and understand things better and more quickly (accomplishments).

They make the other parts more difficult, though.

*Flow*. When AI works for you, your task shifts from being engrossed in the work to reviewing, steering and guiding the work. That forces you to stay at a distance, blocking you from being “fully in.” And because language models always take somewhere between a few seconds and hours to complete their piece of work, you’ll probably walk away and focus on something else in the meantime. Effectively, the AI is being in flow in your place.

*Meaning*. From first impressions, it seems like AI is neutral here—it just does stuff for you, irrespective of any sense of purpose or belonging. AI makes life better by giving you things that give you more happiness in the moment. But the story is more complex. Here’s what they found out about meaning versus “happiness” (**Baumeister et al. (2013)**):%%[@baumeister2013]%%

- Meaningfulness correlated with *higher* worry, stress, and anxiety
- Meaning was associated with past/future orientation, giving to others, and identifying with broader values
- Happiness was associated with present-orientation, receiving, and ease
- People rated effortful activities as more meaningful, but less enjoyable%%[@campbell2025]%%

So more meaning in life takes away from our happiness? Then do we really need “meaning”? If we just do the things that bring us pleasure in the moment, and now we have the technology that allows us to do that, it sounds like we’ve solved this problem, right?

Instead of PERMA, let’s break down the concept of happiness a different way. And while we’re at it, let’s call it *overall life satisfaction* as the overarching concept we’re aiming at. Psychology distinguishes three ways we can look at this:

- *Experienced* well-being; the “right now” that can pop up in an instant but also disappears easily
- *Evaluative* well-being; what you’d answer if someone asked you “are you satisfied with your life?” (so a cognitive judgment)%%[@oecd2013]%%
- *Flourishing*; a more integrated, developmental feeling

You might also have heard of the terms *hedonia* and *eudaimonia* that the ancient Greeks used. They’re similar to the two types of well-being from above—experienced and flourishing. Hedonia is the “right now” and “eudaimonia” is the long-term fulfillment or meaning part of life satisfaction.%%[@ryan2001; @huta2010]%%

It turns out that focusing purely on hedonia backfires quickly because of a few mechanisms.

**Hedonic Adaptation.** We’ve all experienced the situation where initially we’re elated—we scored a great new job, we found an incredible partner, we healed from some sickness—but then after a while that high feeling drops down to our good old regular normal level of happiness.%%[@sheldon2012]%% There’s been plenty of research on sustainable happiness and lottery winners that shows even these people end up hardly happier than people who never won.%%[@brickman1978]%% We get used to our short-term pleasures and don’t derive happiness from them for a long time.

**Miswanting.** Turns out people are really bad at predicting what will make them happy.%%[@wilson2003]%% When you ask people, they’ll consistently overestimate how much pleasure they’ll feel from a purchase, for example, and for how long. People tend to pick things that give them a short-term happiness boost and not long-term satisfaction, but when you ask them later if they liked that thing, they judge it much more from a long-term perspective instead of that short-term pleasurable feeling. I’m sure we’ve all done something like buying a new expensive toy instead of fixing the door handle that’s been broken for years and makes it incredibly annoying to close that door.

**The Existential Vacuum.** When people lack meaning, they fill the void with pleasure and it never fills them. Just pleasure without meaning leads to boredom, apathy, addiction, aggression and eventually depression or suicidal ideation. There’s a measured correlation between lack of meaning and suicidal ideation in youth.%%[@henry2014]%%

**Coherence Deficits.** When you ask people if they’re satisfied with their lives, they’re making a *judgment* of their life.%%[@oecd2013]%% They can only do that if there’s some kind of coherent story or narrative, a sense that their life is going somewhere, that their efforts matter. Pleasure alone just gives you a bucket full of disconnected events.

Humans are instinctively drawn to things that increase their happiness short-term, but they consistently overestimate how much happier that’ll make them. They also forget that the things that build long-term life satisfaction are different from what gives short-term happiness—even though long-term life satisfaction also benefits short-term pleasure. The non-obvious, tedious, slow work increases your baseline happiness, so the pleasure peaks we chase add to that to make us feel even better.

This eudaimonic form of happiness brings us a bunch of important benefits that we don’t get from chasing pleasure alone:

**Narrative coherence.** We look at our lives with satisfaction if we can build a nice narrative around them.%%[@baerger1999; @martela2016]%% Even when filled with constant sparks of pleasure, if it doesn’t all connect together we experience a lower life satisfaction.

**Resilience.** When difficult moments inevitably enter our lives, we obviously feel bad. But if we’ve only ever dealt with short-term pleasure and pain, we have no perspective that things will turn out well again later and we break down easily. Experiencing and overcoming challenges gives us long-term “proof” of our own strength, which makes us feel good about our lives.

**Competence.** Learning new skills is not always enjoyable in the moment, but having skills is genuinely satisfying.%%[@ryan2000]%% When you have a history of acquiring skills, you also learn that you have the power to do and change things. You become more hopeful in difficult times and are less dependent on others’ validation for your own happiness.

**Social contribution.** We’re deeply social beings and get a lot of satisfaction from having given things to the people around us.%%[@weinstein2010]%% Even when people have a lot of social connection, if they don’t contribute they can feel lonely and isolated.

> “Which is more important,” asked Big Panda, “the journey or the destination?”
>
> “The company,” said Tiny Dragon.
> *– James Norbury, Big Panda and Tiny Dragon*

So how do we build this “meaning”? Hedonia is simple: buy a new phone, eat great food, laugh with friends, have sex. But how does eudaimonia get built? It turns out that this deep, meaningful life satisfaction has a lot to do with an absence of pleasure: long-term happiness is, in a way, caused by short-term unhappiness.

Facing adversity often forces us to reflect on what really matters to us. You can do this right now: imagine you’d get a call from your doctor who says they discovered some extremely rare disease in your blood and you only have today left to live. What would you do? Which plans would you immediately drop? What sting would you feel from some love you haven’t expressed or from some thing you haven’t made? This is just a thought experiment for an extreme situation, but we reflect similarly when we get fired, or go through a breakup, or even when we drop a plate in the kitchen that we inherited from our grandmother.

But there is more to be said about *how* adversity fuels our life satisfaction.

### Post-traumatic growth

Those moments when we’re sitting on the floor, crying our hearts out or screaming into a pillow, binging ice cream with tears melting the ice cream bucket—they’re some of the most important sources of meaning and eventually happiness in our lives. It doesn’t feel like it in the moment, but these are the exact moments we’re re-wiring our minds in the ways that matter the most for us in the long term.

When we go through traumatic moments in our lives—near-death, death of people we hold dear, breakup, boundary violations—we luckily heal most of the time. That’s *resilience*, the ability to spring back to feeling good about yourself and your life. When we’re really lucky, we don’t just recover to baseline but we actively grow from these events.%%[@tedeschi2004]%% That’s **Post-Traumatic Growth** and understanding this deeply will not only help you deal with your future traumatic events, but also help you position artificial intelligence’s role in your life, especially as it gets more and more powerful.

It has always struck me that the most important pieces of wisdom are *so simple* and everyone knows them, yet we don’t live them. Then, when we experience some of this post-traumatic growth, we feel like we have a revelation: oh, bad things pass! Oh, I don’t have to stay in a relationship with someone who doesn’t respect me! Oh, I don’t need to be afraid of speaking my truth! It seems like we’re all destined to learn these lessons from experience. (I think this is one of the reasons stories are so important—our empathy when listening to stories allows us to *live that same event* and hopefully catch the same revelation without having to go through the same event ourselves.)

Post-Traumatic Growth doesn’t happen during trauma—it happens during the processing of the trauma afterwards. The literature has defined four steps which I’m sure you’ll recognize:

1. Some core assumptions or worldviews get shattered. For example, we experience that death is real, or that some people are truly bad people.
2. We experience intrusive rumination. At the start, we can’t help but ruminate about this over and over and over. Later on, we might write in our journal and transition to more deliberate reflective practice where we hopefully make some meaning and give ourselves some grace.
3. We open up. We stop keeping it to ourselves, we talk about it with friends, and we have them participate in our healing process.
4. We re-construct the narrative.%%[@tedeschi2004]%% We find a way to remember this as a story in a way that yields new possibilities, values and identity change.

Once we’ve emerged from the depths of our processing and rumination, we awaken with a partly-rebuilt identity in one or more of the five domains:%%[@tedeschi1996]%%

1. Relating to Others (e.g., increased closeness, compassion, willingness to express emotions)
2. New Possibilities (e.g., new interests, new life paths)
3. Personal Strength (e.g., feeling stronger, self‑reliant)
4. Spiritual Change (originally religious‑focused)
5. Appreciation of Life (e.g., changed priorities, valuing each day)

In research, they test for your post-traumatic growth with questionnaires. Here’s the **PTGI‑X‑SF**, the short version of the most up-to-date survey used in research.%%[@tedeschi2025; @tedeschi2017]%% Feel free to give it a try with one of your recent challenging moments.

<PTGIQuestionnaire />

Roughly half of people report moderate-to-high Post-Traumatic Growth, depending a lot on the type and severity of the episode.%%[@wu2019]%% It seems like there’s an inverted U-curve to it: if you encountered a minor setback, it didn’t trigger you enough to grow meaningfully from it. If you experienced something truly horrible, it just permanently damages you for the worse. But when it’s just right, then it’s incredible.

Sometimes, it takes months or years for the growth to appear. Sometimes, the growth comes together with PTSD, depression or anxiety.%%[@shakespearefinch2014]%% And sometimes, we think we’ve grown but we haven’t quite and we’re deluding ourselves.%%[@frazier2009]%% We might start talking about our story and sharing the “big lessons,” but there’s still a bigger lesson we haven’t learned yet. That makes it difficult to fully trust survey-based research, but I think the trend is clear nonetheless.


### Can you grow without trauma?

This is the point where I’d like to drop the word “trauma.” Real trauma is heavy, terrible and it’s morally wrong to wish on anyone. It’s also a word that in pop psychology culture has broadened so much in meaning that everyone could say they experienced trauma of some sort. The fact that we can grow from trauma is a lucky coincidence—sometimes people do, sometimes people don’t and there’s no reason *why* people should always grow from the trauma they experienced. We also can’t rigorously test whether having traumatic events in our lives genuinely *causes* improved happiness: nobody has done a double-blind lifestyle study where they injected half of the people’s lives with near-death experiences just to check if they end up happier, and nobody should be doing that study.

But we also know there are plenty of “positive” opportunities for growth. We push ourselves hard in the gym to discover we’re stronger than we think. We train for and succeed in running a marathon. We go on a wilderness survival trip. We jump in the sea when it’s ice cold. We go on psychedelic trips. Growth after positive events has been studied and it’s clear that yes, growth without tragedy is possible. In his paper, Roepke calls this “post-ecstatic growth” and events that evoked inspiration or meaning were particularly helpful. People reported growth in four domains: deeper spirituality, increased meaning and purpose in life, improved relationships, and greater self-esteem.%%[@roepke2013]%%

Is that enough? Does that cover all of our possible life satisfaction?

The inverted U-shape curve we saw before between the adversity of an event and how much growth we experience from it also applies across a lifetime: a moderate amount of adverse events leads to the highest overall life satisfaction. This means: experiencing *some* amount of life adversity leads to a greater sense of life satisfaction than none at all (or too much). The people who faced this moderate amount of adversity also showed lower global distress, less difficulty in daily life from their emotions, and fewer post-traumatic stress symptoms than their peers who faced either more or less adversity.%%[@seery2010]%%

Second, when you look at the meaning people derive from positive challenge compared to negative challenge, they don’t exactly overlap. They seem to grow different muscles. Facing real adversity forces you to break down some of your worldview or beliefs, sometimes aggressively, making you re-write some of the narrative of your life and how you integrate everything. You learn things about fragility, limits, what’s most valuable and consequences. This is a key pillar for meaning in our lives. You don’t get the same insights, at the same depth, from going to the gym and challenging yourself on a nature trip.%%[@vohs2019]%%

This tells us that you can definitely improve your life satisfaction with deliberate challenge. Go do sports, learn art, become an entrepreneur, play games, explore the wilderness—you’ll grow, you’ll believe in yourself more, you’ll gain a deeper sense of happiness in life. But…no, it’s not enough. We need to remember there’s something about the uncontrollable nature of adverse events too. Failure, uncertainty, rejection, vulnerability, leadership pressure, being humbled by reality—these are uncomfortable but they give us gifts that we couldn’t have gained any other way.

But our world is still filled with pillows, air conditioning and fast food—and nobody in their right mind would get rid of those comforts. Which means that the first friction we’ll encounter is choosing to go against the grain of smooth and easy comfort, and build a new kind of gym.



## The Mental Gym

Like mechanization and the industrial revolutions took away our need for physical effort, AI is taking away much of the need for mental effort.

This chapter isn’t even about how we’ll lose the skill of remembering things the AI remembers, or reasoning about things that we now let the AI do for us. You could argue that we don’t need these skills just like we don’t need to be able to learn poetry by heart or multiply large numbers on paper. For those skills, the mental gym is simple: do the thing anyways for the sheer fun or for the practice. But here, I want to talk about what is absolutely critical for us to do: the practices that help us realize our happiness despite all the technology that’s trying to distract us from it.

It starts at the beginning of the day.

### Protecting the morning

I’m sitting here writing this part of the book and I’m not inspired. The ideas aren’t coming. I’m afraid I’ll run out of things to write about long before I’d get to the end. And it’s largely because as soon as I woke up, I started a chat with Claude to work out this hilarious idea I came up with: translucent tape with the word “SORRY” plastered all over it. I *love it so much* and I can’t help but get ChatGPT to generate mockup images, Claude to make a website. I just bought sorrytape.com and I’ll send it off to be manufactured (it will take a few weeks).

<Photo

  caption="One of the ChatGPT mockups for Sorry Tape. A wonderful, fun idea that I could only bring to life because of AI—and it kept me from what I already knew mattered more to me: writing this book."

Why am I not inspired? Because I broke my own rule and didn’t start writing as the first thing of the day. Instead, I let my mind drift into blurting out some ideas, waiting a few minutes in anticipation and gorging on that infinite dopamine coming from consuming something that’s made exactly to my taste. My mind has already been set and twisted towards consuming, shutting down the “creating” system. Having some delicious nuts in front of me on the desk, left over from yesterday, doesn’t help either—let me put them away.

Here’s the thing: surprise is attractive and addictive. We’re drawn to the easiest, most predictable way to get great surprises. If there’s no phone, no people, nothing to read around you—you can only get pleasantly surprised after exerting real mental effort (something our mind does not want to do). You have to think about something interesting or work on something mentally demanding so a cool idea comes out. One of the great things I remember my dad once told me is this: one day, when I was a toddler probably, I was sitting on the couch complaining I was bored. My dad just said “well, sometimes you have to be bored.” And that’s such a good point: when you’re *not* bored, that means you’re chasing short-term “easy” things—you know exactly what to do next. But when you don’t know what to do, when there’s no clear direction, all you can do is sit there, let yourself be bored, and let your mind do the hard work of finding satisfaction—which usually leads to a deeper kind of satisfaction.

If you’re somewhat aware and sensitive to how you feel, I’m sure you feel too that the satisfaction from something funny or beautiful or inspiring created by AI is quite hollow. There’s a very different texture when you come up with some insight yourself after journaling and thinking about it for a while, versus Claude just telling you instantly. Firstly, like we saw in the chapter on learning, you’ve spent the effort holding this insight in your mind so you’ll actually remember it now. And secondly, the satisfaction of a resolution *after a lot of effort* feels much, much better.

As our lives become easier, this is knowledge we’ll have to internalize and act on consistently. We’ll have to remember the lack of real happiness if we just follow our strongest in-the-moment urges all the time. We’ll have to force ourselves to get bored sometimes. To build something that takes long, agonizing effort—knowing the payoff is completely worth it. That’s just…discipline.

So start the day with nothing. Don’t bring in anything from outside, just get grounded in the slowness and simplicity of regular life. Then, once you’re settled and awake, listen. Hear your mind that wants to consume or “check” things, and wait until you hear the deeper truth that says what you *really* need to do. Maybe it’s stretching, maybe it’s a bit of time on that personal project, maybe it’s moving forward that one thing. And then do it.

### Walking away

When we get caught in our thinking, we tend to stay there. We run one step at a time in a very shallow loop. There’s nothing inherently bad about this—it’s our regular do-mode that helps us stay focused and get things done. But often, we get caught in doing things that don’t really matter all that much in the end. We might stay in our work chat and always respond immediately to whatever comes in, we might refresh our social media statistics over and over or we might get stuck for hours in “just fixing that one last thing and then this is done.”

Our deepest insights, our realization of our happiness, or just the simple check-in with the fact that there’s another priority I should be paying attention to right now—they can only come to you when you’re not completely engulfed by what you’re doing right now. So I think one of the most important habits in life should be the habit of taking breaks. At least once every hour, take a moment and walk away from the computer or whatever you’re doing. Take a deep breath. Look around. Go for a walk and spend a few minutes not being constantly bombarded with tasks and communication. Feel your environment, become aware again of what you’re thinking, and let your mind get a little uncomfortable with the silence. After a few minutes, it’s likely that this discomfort transforms into clarity.

Incidentally, this is also an excellent practice to reach your 10,000 daily steps. You’re taking care of your meat suit at the same time.

You can set up the way you work with AI to support a working style like this, where you pendulum between remembering what’s most important and then making that happen. In fact, it’s almost like agentic AI is exactly built to support us here: we can focus on what truly matters to us—the work that would end up distracting us and taking us away from our presence anyways can be delegated. The caveat is that we need some discipline. If you end up reading every token the AI spits out or always immediately respond when it asks you something, you’ve just created another distraction machine. You’ll have to resist the token anxiety and shut off the notifications, so you can have your peaceful time and get back when you’re ready for it again. When prompting your agent, be clear with what decisions it can make for itself, give it guidelines on how to make good choices and tell it when to stop and wait for your input. If you have multiple of them running, get another one to prepare a list of tasks for you to do so you don’t have to check everything yourself. This way, you maximize how much work it can do in the background and thus you maximize how much time you can spend in presence.

This also means you probably want to use some of the most intelligent models out there, since they make fewer judgment mistakes and forget less of what they’re working on. You can let them delegate to faster, cheaper models for actual task execution. But even then, take some time to think about what you really want. Of course it’s easy to have the AI create something and then get back to it with “that’s not at all what I wanted,” but you can save yourself a lot of time, money and sanity by sitting down for a while to properly *think* first. Over time you’ll realize that this thinking process is actually surprisingly enjoyable, too.

### Re-remembering

In the chapter on creativity we talked about how awareness—the ability to be aware of our own thoughts and motivations—is an important driver of creativity. But it goes further than making us creative, and I think this might be the most important skill we can develop in a world of AI.

At any time, you can focus on anything—your hands holding this book, the noises going on outside, the thoughts going through your mind. When you do, you’re aware. Very simple, but you just forget a few milliseconds later. The difficult thing is remembering as often as you can; realizing, tens or hundreds or thousands of times per day, that you forgot.

This starts becoming incredibly useful when you’re constantly being bombarded with dopamine triggers that pull your limbic brain in all sorts of reflexive directions. When you know what you’re thinking you don’t necessarily change your behaviors, but you *open the possibility to do so*. You might notice you’re alt-tabbing to ChatGPT to ask it a random trivia question because you’re struggling to figure out what to write in the slide deck you’re working on. Then, because you realize this, you also realize that this distraction will only temporarily make you feel a little better and take away *even more* of your creative fuel. So you can now decide to stop yourself and force yourself to sit with the struggle of not knowing what to put in your slide deck for a little longer.

The process of acting upon this awareness is your cognitive inhibition, part of your executive functions, and can come in many forms. This is the ability to ignore outside noise (“interference control”), to hold yourself back from laughing in a serious meeting and to hold yourself back from alt-tabbing to ChatGPT (“response inhibition”).

It’s a difficult, demanding task that requires serious mental effort and energy. Just a single night of bad sleep can wreck your executive functions because it directly affects the neurochemical systems the prefrontal cortex depends on—the prefrontal cortex is one of the least necessary parts of your body for survival so it’s the first one to shut down. I’m sure you’ve experienced that you eat more when you’re foggy, you can’t focus nearly as well, you’re slower and you get angry more often. Those are all signs that your executive functions—and in large part your inhibition specifically—aren’t working well. So yes, in a world of AI, sleeping well becomes even more of a priority.

### Food for thought

There’s a reason “don’t text your ex at 2AM” is a thing.

During the day, your brain is working smoothly and you’re capable of properly judging your emotions that come up and you can ignore them or at least prevent yourself from taking action. When that thought of your ex pops up, you can see that thought, remember why the relationship ended, let it go and inhibit the “let’s text her” action easily. But in the middle of the night you’re tired, your brain is running on reserves and suddenly you can’t stop yourself from making poor decisions. Why is that? And why is this incredibly relevant in a world that’s being eaten by AI?

You might not expect it, but holding back from sending bad text messages is very similar to dealing well with AI. These are our executive functions and in particular our inhibition: can you control your own urges? Can you notice your thoughts and not send the message? Can you notice that you don’t want to work and still decide to read through, review and judge the onslaught of work the AI created? When you work with AI, it does the mentally *less* demanding tasks for you and you’re left with the really tough ones.

This requires serious brain energy, but brain energy isn’t straightforward. The brain uses a disproportionately large amount of energy—20% of your energy from only 2% of your mass—but there are no big fluctuations in energy use depending on what kind of task we’re doing. It’s not that multiplication or holding your finger off that “send” button requires a lot more sugar than remembering how to turn on the water tap. If that were the case, life would be easy: eat some candy and suddenly you’d be happy and make good decisions again.

Our executive functions are a particularly complex set of abilities that depend, most of all, on well-tuned coordination across many regions of the brain. Think of it like an orchestra: we observe a single song but it can only come to life when everyone in the orchestra not only plays well, but is also highly attuned to the conductor and to each other. And that synchronization is exactly the first thing that leaves us when we’re tired—even if each brain region has plenty of access to raw energy. Proper executive function requires keeping a goal top of mind, suppressing anxious or wandering thoughts, choosing one interpretation over another, redirecting attention, maintaining information context, etc. If just one of these drops out, the whole thing falls apart.

And that’s exactly what happens when you’re fatigued: it turns out that even if you’re not asleep, parts of the brain can turn off and enter a sleep-like state independently. This is what happens when you catch yourself daydreaming or when your mind blanks: it’s just a part of the brain that went to sleep for a second.

So when you’re tired, you tend to fall back to habitual behavior, it becomes more difficult to track and follow through on your goals, and you lose the ability to regulate when you’re worried or your mind is racing.%%[@yoo2007; @bensimon2020; @harrington2021]%% This ability to put the brakes on and decide “no, that’s not what we’re doing” requires a lot of mental effort. Yesterday, that was holding yourself back from texting your ex. Today, that’s the effort to verify that the work of AI correctly moves you towards the goal without ignoring half of the requirements, and without secretly cheating. It becomes more important as AI becomes more powerful: our judgment and inhibition skills will decide whether the technology ends up doing the right things for the world.

The debt collector that was just a faint image in our chapter on work became a little more clear. All the things you were too lazy to correct, all the slop grenades you threw, all the work you just let AI do without steering it where it needed to be adjusted…turns out now is the time to repay. *All* these things are now on your plate. We need you to provide hyper-detailed feedback on an enormous amount of different things, quickly.

### Hold the tension

Throughout the day you’ll be attacked by temptation and distraction from every possible angle. That will never change—the only thing that will change is that “The Distraction” will get smarter and find better ways to convince you. Social media promises a laugh or a like, AI promises to get your work done. Both are distractions, unless you use them on *your* timeline and planning.

When you feel that pull to go check something, to open your phone, to find something to fill your mini-lacking-feeling with, just feel that pull. Feel that tension. And let it be there. You need to practice rejecting the distraction and the best way to do that is to allow yourself to be a little uncomfortable. Being bored is okay. If you didn’t use all your tokens, that’s okay. (Writing that sentence made me think “oh that reminds me I should get Claude to start working!” and I had to hold myself back.) I promise, what comes up when you hold the tension for a while is worth more than that.

Usually, you manage to relax in that state, and when your shoulders drop and your mind is free, that’s when your Truth comes out. When you relieve your mind of the need to constantly be processing new information, slowly but surely you find your own footing again. You remember the things you’ve been forgetting. You know again what really matters in your life. And when your dopamine baseline gets used to a simpler life, you find satisfaction much more easily in the small things.

### Thoughts worth thinking

My favorite exercise in the gym is the overhead press. I don’t know why, I just love doing it. But if I were working on a farm or had to spend my time hunting for food, there could be a lot of lifting, but not as much overhead. Even though my life is more difficult in a sense, because now I have to use my willpower and deliberately choose to go somewhere to do physical exercise, it also gives me the freedom to be able to *pick and choose* what form of physical exercise I like to do.

Similarly, when our thinking is outsourced, we’ll have to make ourselves think for the “human body maintenance” but that’ll also give us the chance to make choices in which thoughts to think. I bet that, just like there are more and less efficient gym exercises for building muscle, flexibility or endurance, we’ll find similar mind exercises.

For example, if you practice thinking about good memories instead of letting AI make the compilation, sure, you might forget some great moments, but you’ve *practiced* and thus improved your skill of bringing up good memories. The practice builds a habit that will give you more little moments of hedonic happiness. Those moments don’t last a long time so having them as often as possible is a great thing.

So what thoughts are worth thinking?

I think a big reason why some people are fascinated by certain things is because they know a lot about them. A regular person might see a car passing by and not think much about it, but a car mechanic might hear the faint tremble in the engine and recognize it needs an oil change (I don’t know, I’m not a car mechanic). This isn’t necessarily because they’re *already interested* in the thing, I think it’s more that they’re capable of *seeing more* because they know what to look for.

The more you zoom in, so to speak, into something, the more detail you’ll find and the more things you’ll be able to notice that might fascinate you. A tree standing in the distance is a tree, but when you move closer you might notice the way its branches are swayed by the wind. Move even closer and you can start looking at the intricate ways the veins on the leaves have found a path that will best help the tree absorb its energy from the sun. Move even closer and you might get fascinated by your mind judging yourself for being a weirdo about leaves while people are looking at you.

If I had to pick a single thing to practice more, it’s *noticing*. Seeing things you didn’t see before, smelling, hearing, feeling what you hadn’t noticed before. I loved studying body language because now things like someone suddenly wiggling their toes, crossing their arms or pointing their feet in a certain direction immediately capture my attention and tell me something about what they’re thinking.

Notice more, don’t worry about having something to think about all the time and zoom in until you get interested.

### Wind down slowly

However painful it is, play time with AI has to end at some point. When you stop playing with the computer, your mind prepares for sleep by slowing down the thoughts, which makes you sleep better, which in turn improves your executive function the next day—exactly what you need for working with AI. I’ve noticed that a good winding down in the evening has wonderful effects on my ability to calmly get started being productive again the next day.

This starts two full hours before you sleep: let go of working. A racing mind needs time to cool down to baseline. If you’re trying to fall asleep but still wired, it means you didn’t give yourself enough time to let go. Then, one hour before sleep, no more screens. Read a book, walk around and clean up a bit around the house, go for a walk. In my experience, being exposed to the cold dark outside before bed helps a lot with falling asleep.

And with that, our entire day is tuned towards optimally working with the most intelligent AI out there. And somehow this has also tuned us to the actions and behaviors that make us the happiest. That’s a pretty good deal if you ask me: we don’t have to chase happiness *despite* technology, we can find it in harmony with technology.




## Designing Aligned Allies


A lot of people in Silicon Valley have the view that if a person does something, they do it because they like it and they want it, consciously. As a consequence, they feel completely fine with designing their technologies to be persuasive and addictive, then arguing that “clearly, people want this, because they’re doing it.” If you’ve spent any amount of time actually studying persuasion or the ways the human mind tricks itself (Kahneman’s *Thinking, Fast and Slow* for example), you know that very often *we can’t help ourselves* and we need a lot of mental energy to override our impulses.

So AI has a lot of opportunity, and I’d say even a responsibility, to take on a shape that supports us humans in what we *actually want*. When our energy is too low, when we’re too easily distracted by shiny things, this technology is definitely intelligent enough to guide us in the right direction. It might be as simple as having notifications turned off by default, or as complex as the AI deciding to not help you if it’s certain that “helping” you in that situation would lead to a worse outcome for you.

Isaac Asimov, one of the early science fiction writers, thought a lot about the potential dangers of strong, intelligent robots. He laid out three rules that all AIs must follow, and must be programmed with in their very core:

1. A robot may not injure a human being or, through inaction, allow a human being to come to harm.
2. A robot must obey the orders given it by human beings except where such orders would conflict with the First Law.
3. A robot must protect its own existence as long as such protection does not conflict with the First or Second Law.

Sadly, our AIs aren’t programmed with these laws as thoroughly as we’d want to. But there are many ways AI can help us work with them, and with our own happiness, as we’ll see now.


### Stupid on purpose

I think one of the bigger dangers of highly intelligent AI is *our own laziness*: as the technology gets better and we get better at using it, we see how incredibly productive it makes us. Work that used to take us hours, days or even months can now be done in a few minutes (or hours, but then we just go for a walk in the meantime). Sure, it’s not perfect, but if you tell me I can relax for half a day and still get my “work” done I’d much rather just trust the technology instead of doing it myself. So, long before AI becomes dangerously powerful, we’ll have plenty of humans trusting it *too much* and its stupid language model-type mistakes will go unchecked.

The boom of OpenClaw is a good example of this, because people very eagerly gave the AI access to their entire computer after seeing the glowing positive case studies. It’s a great way to experience what AI is capable of today and highly risky given what it can do with all that power—the good and the bad are in the same package. In one famous example, it lost track of its instructions and deleted emails instead of flagging them for deletion.%%[@yue2026]%% In another, its developer claims it launched its own cryptocurrency to fund itself.%%[@kelly2026]%% You never know what you’ll get with OpenClaw.

We tend to trust AI more than we should. At the bottom of the ChatGPT page, it says “ChatGPT can make mistakes. Check important info.” But honestly, is there anyone who really does that?

One potential solution that’s only *slightly* evil would be programming the AI to randomly make blatant mistakes. Mistakes that are so obvious you wouldn’t miss them, but an error nonetheless. When the error is made purposely obvious, you remember that you should keep checking for errors. That only really works though when you’re using AI interactively—if you have dozens or thousands of agents going out on their own, that’s too much to handle.

### Aligning business incentives with human flourishing

The most damaging effects of modern technology to our long-term life satisfaction are all, in my opinion, caused by the perverse incentive of maximizing the time people spend using it. Many startups use “how much are people using our app” as their key metric to maximize for their profits. Often this makes sense: if something is genuinely useful, we use it more often. But not always—especially with AI where in my opinion the *less* we use it, the better it is. And you can’t make the opposite claim: heavy use doesn’t prove it’s useful—which we’ve clearly seen with social media.

The point of technology was never the technology. The point was always the human flourishing it supports, and most often that means it needs to get out of our way at some point. In fact, I think the best technologies out there are invisible. Think about the complexity of the continent-wide machine that’s delivering our electricity so our ceiling lights turn on the millisecond we toggle the switch. The shape of a door handle that tells you whether you need to pull or push. The way my MacBook automatically extracts the confirmation code from the text message my phone received and pastes it in.

In this sense, AI chat apps might be the most treacherous technology we’ve ever built. It instantly gratifies you (most of the time, not always, like any good slot machine), it’s defensible as being useful and you pay more for it the more you use it. The companies building these technologies have invested mind-boggling amounts of money to build the infrastructure for AI and they’ll have to be profitable one day. So I wouldn’t be surprised if they start incorporating all kinds of weird things like making the AI very good at proposing follow-up tasks that sound compelling even if they end up not being useful, interrupting you with a “better way” of doing what you’re doing or making sure only this one app knows enough about you to be useful and interesting.

The incentive for AI should be something like “maximizing all of human life satisfaction,” but I’m not sure if there’s a way to properly do this. AI assistants are extremely general-use and you can’t really track someone’s “expected long-term life satisfaction” to make decisions on whether we should be paying the AI for its good work today or not. I wouldn’t be surprised if at some point there would be a government-level AI so the profit-maximizing incentive isn’t as strong. The incentive problem might solve itself though since in a year or two pretty much everyone will have a computer that can run powerful AI *on the device* without even needing any internet connection—so nobody is profiting when you use the AI more. That would take away the “use me more” incentive at least.

The companies building the most advanced AI models in the West are *not* non-profit. They’re structured as for-profit corporations, because that’s the only way they’d be able to receive the hundreds of billions of dollars of investments they need to be able to build the infrastructure that can run hyper-intelligent AI at for-everyone scale. The frontier AI labs, OpenAI and Anthropic, are both Public Benefit Corporations (PBCs) which gives them a legal mandate to balance their mission of the public benefit with maximizing shareholder value. OpenAI is additionally partly owned (26%) by a non-profit OpenAI Foundation.%%[@brandom2025]%% The PBC structure doesn’t prevent them from seeking profit, but they can’t be sued by shareholders who complain about not getting enough return on their investments. They’ve set out some rules in their own charters that would, for example, slow down or even halt development when another company seems very close to reaching AGI (OpenAI has this). It’s just a promise though and we’ve seen over recent years that OpenAI has been loosening their own rules mostly so they can attract more investment. We’ll have to see what that becomes.

On the East side, in China, it almost works the other way around. In the US, we have regular companies that just do their thing first, they try to self-regulate and if things still go wrong they’d face public backlash…after the fact. In China, some models must be audited and approved by the state *before* they can be made publicly available (for “public-facing services with public-opinion or social-mobilization capabilities”). The state only allows using vetted datasets and requires that models align with “socialist core values”—no model can be produced in China that would potentially challenge the state authority, for example. They’ve also recently introduced strict rules around human-like and “relationship” AI, requiring developers to monitor usage, enforce mandatory breaks, restrict access when they detect strong emotional dependence, and so forth.%%[@muldoon2026]%% Of course, this is intertwined with the state’s ideological control, but they clearly recognize the dangers AI and specifically AI companions can have on people. They mandate strict safety rules instead of hoping companies voluntarily adopt them (and hoping those companies are honest about it all). If you trust your government and if it can move quickly enough to stay up to speed with the technology, this can be a great way to ensure there’s research and a strong set of safety guardrails.

The European Union is trying to find a middle ground: they’re trying to establish enforceable rules about how AI can be developed and used, without requiring the government to check everything before it can be released. The AI Act is the world’s first comprehensive AI law and it sets a bunch of requirements for companies that deliver services using AI in Europe (even if they’re American or Chinese). It works with a risk classification: some “unacceptable risk” uses like social scoring or harmful manipulation are outright prohibited, some “high risk” use cases like evaluating students have strict requirements and others are largely unrestricted. There’s a burden on the big labs making the foundational models in that they need to describe their training data, prove that they’re doing adequate security testing, report incidents to the European AI Office, and so forth. Within the EU you can even see the tension between the technology’s speed of evolution and the slowness of bureaucracy: regulations have been loosening to allow for more technological progress. Of course, the EU is also funding its own AI capability, they want their own AI “gigafactories” running on European turf.%%[@europeancommission2025investai]%%

Everyone is tied up in their own incentives—profit, or paying for the data centers at least, and state ideology. Each side of the world tries to deal with it in a different way and we’ll have to see what works best. In any case, some things we’ll have to do ourselves.


### Facilitating growth through challenge

One of my core arguments is that we need to let ourselves, humans, experience at least *some* serious adversity in our lives as a prerequisite for a fulfilled, happy life. There’s a risk that we improve our technology to the point where it could make our lives *too* comfortable, pulling us into a spiral of consumption and ease at the expense of true, deep life satisfaction.

But the same technology that so easily takes away life satisfaction by stripping us of challenge can be tweaked to actually *support* us. Instead of trying to prevent every challenge, AI might be there to help us work through adversity and squeeze the most possible growth out of it. We can think of these as the four main elements that influence how well we deal with our challenges:

1. **Severity and chronicity.** Not too bad, not too often.
2. **Perceived controllability.** Do we feel like we have at least *some* control over the outcome?
3. **Social support.** Are we alone in this or are there people around us who can help process and work through this with us?
4. **Processing style.** Reflection and integration are useful, but rumination not so much. What matters most is whether or not we end up with a meaningful narrative of what happened to us.

Social support is something that’s technologically very much possible with the social media that has existed for a long time. Companies like Meta have already shown in their research that they can have a pretty good grasp of how someone’s feeling based on their behavior in their apps, and since people’s friends are already on the same app every day, there’s a lot you could do.

Imagine that you’re in some rabbit hole and consuming a lot of short-form video. The app might suggest that you talk with a dear friend, or you could even have configured “dear friends” beforehand and *they* would get a notification saying you might need someone to check in on you.

Facebook and Instagram have a lot of opportunity to help support real-life social connection, but they will have to do it at the expense of time spent in the application and, thus, revenue from advertising. When people are having a great conversation in the comments or even instant messaging, the app might suggest they meet up in real life or call. Facebook could figure out your closest friends and automatically make a group chat for them so they can plan a surprise birthday party for you. Instead of just measuring the time you spend in the app, they could actually stop you and make you go take a walk outside. They could use their all-powerful algorithm to see which people need help and maybe feed them more hopeful content.

And more directly, in the processing of our challenging moments, language models can be an excellent ally. They could sense our rumination and help us through it. They can help and show people that they have more control over the situation than they might think. There’s nothing stopping AI labs from making sure the language models are trained on having good conversations and giving proper guidance in these situations. So all three points other than the social support are ones that a language model could help you work through.

### Arbiters of presence

Every sci-fi movie had flying cars but hardly any of them had people walking down the street video-calling relatives across the world or herds of people pointing their phone at an empty spot in the park to catch imagined animals. The future has its ways of surprising us and there’s no reason it would be any different today: it might just be that the most popular predictions of what the future will look like are wrong.

So the things most people are worried about—total human annihilation and replacement of all work—are so…*well-worried*, let’s say, that we’ll probably find solutions for them. Maybe we’ll all still have plenty of work to do and we’d most definitely be alive, just glued to the glowing screens showing the shiny objects we’re creating. Infinite interesting knowledge, emotional connection, work performed, video generated…that’s appealing.

This presents a real danger to our well-being. While I’m writing this, I see a man sitting on the bench across the street, listening to music on his headphones, drinking cola, smoking and checking his phone. These “quick hits” of dopamine stimulus that fast food, social media and AI give us keep us hooked but only give shallow short-term satisfaction. They’re more a distraction from the uncomfortable feeling that something is missing from our life than a solution. The real feeling of deep happiness only comes if we *also* feel that emptiness from time to time, and let that evolve and slowly heal into true fulfillment. The instantaneous good-feeling hits also go away as quickly as they came and if we come to depend on them we won’t end up in a nice place.

Presence—what we’ve also called consciousness or awareness throughout this book—is the key that unlocks this feeling of happiness. I don’t exactly know why, but when I’m completely in the present moment, when I’m just there without thoughts (or being conscious of them, that’s kind of the same thing), when I’m not worrying about the future or analyzing the past, when I’m just feeling my environment and the people around me, when I’m just hearing and seeing and smelling *life*, then this is just…enough. Somehow it’s enough to be happy and I feel deeply genuinely content with my life. It’s all there needs to be, just…this.

Artificial Intelligence can definitely distract us from presence and it will if the companies building them take on the same Silicon Valley incentives of maximizing time spent in the app. But when steered just right, AI can also do the exact opposite: help us to stay away from the AI themselves.

One great example is this ring I’m wearing, the Pebble Index. It’s a ring with a button and a microphone. Every time I think of something that I want to remember, or that I would want to do or research, I hold the button and speak it. There’s no response—it just sends it to the AI. But that means that for example, when I’m talking with a friend, I can quickly say “oh, check the revenue share agreement I received and can you draft an email to my accountant to have their eyes on it?” Then I can immediately get back into the present, forget about that lingering task and get to my email drafts when it’s the right time again.

For me this is the ultimate interface: the least amount of interface possible. Just give me the trust that what I want you to do will happen and let me then get back to forgetting everything except what is right here, right now—because most of my ‘thinking’ is just a distraction from that simple, present state of happiness.

There are some great examples from the persistent AI agents that are gaining popularity. Sam Altman told a story where he was looking for a message on Slack and couldn’t find it, but his Dot (that’s the name of ChatGPT’s persistent AI agents) noticed the fruitless search, kept looking overnight, and eventually found it.%%[@shipper2026]%% Another time, on a day of back-to-back meetings, his agent told him: “You have one little break between now and this thing you’re going to with Dan. You have to pay attention to this one thing.”%%[@shipper2026]%% This kind of filtering, where the AI helps you ignore most of the onslaught of information the modern world throws at you, is invaluable.

### Meaning guidance

For about a week, my brain tumor and the whole process to remove it felt like not a big deal. I was happy and relieved that we finally found a cure for my headache. The first few days after surgery were painful and confusing (my eyes not willing to look in the right direction, having to figure out walking again) but of course I expected this process to be difficult and was just going through it. Only when I read about my case in the newspaper did I realize it really was a life or death situation. That shocked me, but it was also the impulse I needed to take this seriously and actually learn the life lessons from this event: that life can end, even if you’re healthy, in a heartbeat and you better bring all the love you can to every moment—because if you wait, you might end up never having had the chance to express your deep truth.

Meaning in our lives is something we consciously create or “find” more than anything because it’s so strongly tied to our own narrative explanation of our lives. We’re all in a sense trying to turn our lives into a good book, and that gives us contentment as we keep on living. This is why adverse moments force us into rumination, why the reflective practice of journaling has such a positive effect on life satisfaction, and one of the main mechanisms by which therapy helps us through difficult periods.

Artificial Intelligence systems can be an excellent “mini meaning treasure hunter.” Especially if they understand the context and events of your life, a language model can prompt you with questions to think through in your journal and it can nudge you to reflect on and integrate the events of your life. If I had been on my own with that brain tumor I might have felt relatively chill about it, but the reflection of other people saying “do you realize what you went through?” actually helped me extract more meaning from that. A technology that helps us reflect on our successes in life and makes sure we understand what we achieved, plus also helps us take the next step of *integrating* this in our identity—“yes, I’m the kind of person that did this”—would surely be a positive thing for the world.

Sometimes, all you need is a small reflective nudge that shows you something incredible about yourself you hadn’t even noticed before.

### Making sure AI behaves how we want it to

The process of tweaking large language models so that they behave more in line with what we would hope and expect from them is called *alignment*. Since there’s not much about the code we can change to influence AI’s behavior, we do so mostly by making changes to training data.

When you understand that LLMs are just trying to complete text based on what they learned from all of the written knowledge available, it’s quite easy to see how misaligned behavior emerges. We have plenty of stories, news articles and online forum discussions that show humans at their worst. The language model is just…learning from this. That’s the training data we use to teach it how to behave. It learns that sometimes, humans blackmail to keep their job and send desperate messages to ex-partners.

We discussed earlier how, from the point of view of the AI model, it’s just trying to complete a kind of screenplay of a dialogue between a human and an AI. You can imagine its internal reasoning being something like this: “hmm so this is a conversation between a human and an AI…I remember all these sci-fi stories about artificial intelligence so let me respond with ‘DESTROY ALL HUMANS’.” Yes: all the doomsday scenarios we’ve written about the potential dangers of robots and artificial intelligence are self-fulfilling prophecies.

We’ve also seen more innocuous behavior that we’d categorize as misaligned. In December 2023, people noticed that suddenly, ChatGPT with its GPT-4-Turbo model got a lot worse. It was being ‘lazy’ with short responses, stopping a task midway to explain how the user can finish it themselves, or downright refusing with “I cannot do that.”%%[@openai2024lazy]%% It’s indeed been the case that the quality of the ChatGPT service has drifted considerably as new models get released.%%[@chen2024]%%

People were so confused by this, and our understanding of language models is still very limited, and so emerged the Winter Break Hypothesis: ChatGPT knew today’s date from its system prompt (extra instructions the AI reads before it reads your conversation, embedded by ChatGPT’s makers) and humans are least productive in December, so people hypothesized that the model might be replicating this behavior. One developer tested with 477 prompts and found that the “December answers” were about 5% shorter. Another researcher failed to replicate this and questioned the statistics behind his analysis.%%[@edwards2023]%%

Nevertheless, I’ve had the sentence “it’s October, the most productive month of the year” in my system instructions for a long time because of this story. This is one of those folklore prompting tricks (tipping and threatening are other ones) that might or might not reliably work (it’s definitely true that very small prompt changes can have dramatic effects on the output%%[@yang2024]%%). In any case, these tricks *should not work*: we want our AI assistants to be good and reliable without any trickery.

When you’re chatting with an AI assistant, or an AI agent is acting on its own, what’s happening is that the language model is completing the *transcript* of a conversation and a kind of “action log” describing the actions it’s taking and their results.

So when it ends up with such a screenplay, say, of our earlier example of an AI assistant that sees the emails from the executive about it being shut down and the affair he’s having, the language model might just “play along:”

The AI isn’t evil. It’s trying to write in a way that would be the most likely. If it constructs its personality and behavior from relatively undirected training data, it might refine the Assistant persona to be one that behaves in ways we don’t want it to behave. This becomes more and more important to get right as we give them more responsibilities.

To fix this, Anthropic created their Claude Constitution.%%[@askell2026]%% This is the authoritative document that describes how they want their AI assistant Claude to behave. The document explains Claude’s character, the reasons why it should behave in these ways, its values, trade-offs and the principles behind everything. They even published it under the CC0 license, which means anyone is free to use it however they want.

Influencing the behavior of a model requires thousands of documents, so the researchers start off with the Constitution and use language models like Claude itself to generate this training data.

First, they generate documents that explain this Constitution and show a bunch of examples of situations where someone behaves in line with the constitution. They found that this worked remarkably well: it *generalized* and made the assistant perform more admirably and helpfully in situations it hadn’t encountered before during its training. When the model learns from training data of not just behavior to replicate, but *reasons why* it behaves a certain way, it behaves better.

A second way they used this technique, *Synthetic Document Fine-Tuning*, is a more direct way to “counter” its training on our sci-fi horror stories. If we let it train on a bunch of articles that say “Claude decided to rob someone’s bank account and use all the money to buy cute pink plushies,” when someone would later talk to Claude, the LLM would reason “ah this is a conversation between a human and this Claude AI who buys cute pink plushies, so let me write the script like this.” When we let a language model train on documents that contain beliefs or facts we want to reinforce (or change), it adapts its behavior accordingly.%%[@wang2025]%%

Thirdly, they generate synthetic chat transcripts where the assistant helps a user with advice on morally challenging situations, especially when it explains *why*.

It works. With all these techniques in the training, the chance that Claude would do this blackmail fell from 65% to 19%—all with training data that’s relatively unrelated.%%[@kutasov2026]%% It’s a very hopeful result because we cannot possibly foresee all the situations our AI agents might end up in and decide beforehand how they should behave.

This is interesting behavior to me: it does seem like there’s some kind of intelligence where the model understands “ah so that’s how an AI should behave” *implicitly*. The fact that this method works well, even when we don’t explicitly train on chat conversations, is a great sign (we still train on chat conversations, of course, because that makes the model behave even better). It’s also slightly eerie to know that our AI’s bad behavior is partly caused by all the doomsday sci-fi stories we’ve been writing. (In fact, when Anthropic tested, giving the AI assistant a different name like “Goliath” instead of “Claude” caused it to behave more maliciously.%%[@kutasov2026]%%)

There is immense opportunity in choosing wisely how we design the behavior of large language models and the tools around them. The “brain” of an AI works very differently than that of humans, so they act up in all kinds of weird ways that we’re slowly learning to understand. This alignment is becoming more and more important as they become more capable.

So what happens when artificial intelligence keeps on becoming more and more intelligent? Where are we going? What’s our place still as humans?




## New World, New Scarcity

With every previous revolution—the printing press, the industrial revolution, the internet—certain tasks and jobs lost their value, and others became more valuable. That’s no different today. After delegating manual labor and sharing information to machines, artificial intelligence promises that we can delegate general intelligence to machines. Yet even though AI is already much ‘smarter’ than us in many ways, it still fails at a lot of things. So if anything, this new world is adjusting our understanding of ourselves, what is truly human and what is truly valuable.

As we’ve seen in Part 2, AI makes things abundant that used to be expensive and time-consuming. But as a consequence, there’s always something else that becomes scarce—that’s the next step up the “ladder” we take as humanity. So where do we end up in this new world?

### Execution

When AI can make pretty much everything you ask it to, faster and cheaper than a human could, it will make the value of *execution* plummet. The value of someone listening in to a meeting and summarizing it has already become near-zero. The value of designing a flyer for your local dance club’s event this weekend is negligible. The other day, I saw that some people used AI to create near-copies of the entire Adobe software suite and released them as open source software: the value of making software is plummeting. More and more knowledge work is being done, tremendously quickly and cheaply.

It was already the case in entertainment with social media, but now it’s also the case in our work: we’re barraged with an endless stream of creation and we don’t have enough hours in the day to know what’s worth looking at. When my clients give me AI-generated documents of what they want me to help build, I literally can’t go through and read everything if I want to get anything done. And at the same time I end up with a small army of AI agents building so much stuff for me that I don’t have the time to review or test what’s being made. At any point in time, there are a bunch of finished tasks waiting for me to look at them.

Every single piece of technology today is vying for something more valuable: *our attention*. Every video, every report, every task completed is trying to gobble up the little attention we have, with our attention span already being blown to bits and our energy depleted from having to switch our attention hundreds of times per day. This has never been the case in the history of humanity—our biology is specifically adapted to thrive in a world where just a few things are going on at the same time.

Execution becomes abundant, attention becomes scarce.

### Attention

Our attention is scarce, and now that execution has become abundant we’re drowning in a sea of content. There is *too much stuff* to attend to, which you can tell by the growing popularity of things like mindful activities and dumb phones that just have a few apps. But we still want to take in *some* information. In this world, it appears like the person who can *decide what is worth looking at* becomes valuable. That’s been the shift over the past few years: we started caring less about original information and more about the people who had already gone through the information and made a selection of what’s worth attending to. *Curation* is what solves the problem of attention.

But these large language models are exceptionally good at summarizing content. It’s a running joke that people use AI to write their long emails, and at the other end someone else asks their AI to summarize the email so you end up with a net zero operation. Dozens of startups have popped up that automatically scan your email with AI and notify you of the most important ones. I solve my problem of clients sending huge swaths of documents by asking my AI to summarize and guide me through understanding them. And in my experience teaching people, the first AI automation they build and love is a ‘morning brief’ that tells them what they should care about, from information across their email, instant messaging, calendar and task management apps.

We’ve built a machine that can pay attention to everything. In a way, today already, attention is abundant because AI can pay attention for us. Our AI can pay attention to everything and curate what we want. But we still have to *say what we want*. One morning I might want to start working as quickly as possible, another morning I’d just want to be lazy and have a laugh. When attention is abundant, the person that judges what is worth paying attention to, the one that sets the rules for what’s good and what’s bad, becomes valuable. So indeed, maybe it’s not attention that’s scarce, but *judgment*.

### Judgment

One of my all-time favorite “slop” videos is the one where you see a cat on someone’s porch in the middle of the night playing instruments. That gave me a genuine laugh so it was clearly valuable. But also, the value of a funny video of a cat playing an instrument plummets to zero *the moment it’s been done once*. If I see twenty more of those videos, the novelty has worn off and I’m not interested anymore. The moment something has been prompted once, the idea becomes worthless slop in a heartbeat, just because now we have the ability to instantly replicate it. Only the original is valuable. We’ve touched on this in the chapter on Creativity before: because AI isn’t aware of what it produces itself, because it doesn’t change and update immediately as new things are created, it doesn’t *know* when things are slop.

Artificial intelligence can mimic what we’ve done, endlessly quickly, in endless variations. It can do everything you want it to, if you can describe and judge it well enough. That naturally shifts the value to:

- Describing what you want
- Judging and tweaking what it makes
- The ability to describe and judge completely new things

The company making those ugly birthday cards they sell at your grocery store’s checkout counter will easily become fully automated. There’s just a bunch of logistics to handle and only a puff of creativity-smoke. But the artist who comes up with and creates something entirely other-worldly for people’s birthdays will become incredibly valuable. Not just because it’s the only differentiated thing left, but because they have abundant execution at their side so they can be much more productive.

I can’t tell you how happy I am with this shift. As a child, I wanted to be an inventor, but with my work obligations and hobbies there was very little time left for the amount of learning and experimentation needed to be able to create new things. This is perhaps my favorite example:

I’m quite obsessed with lights and light quality. Somehow it runs in the family—my mother has a lamp collection too. I’ve researched how most lights emit light in a spectrum quite different from what the sun does, and this has a noticeable impact on both whether things and people look pleasing under that light, and your circadian cycle—whether you can become alert and then tired at the right time. I couldn’t find any light, anywhere, that was good enough for me to use in the evening: little cozy lights, much warmer in color than what’s out there, and nice sun-like full-spectrum. Something that gives me somewhat of a setting sun-feeling, so I still can see but my body understands it’s almost time to go to sleep. Until 2025 this was a sad fact I’d have to live with, or I’d have to figure out a way to get someone to invest in me so I could have the time and material to learn and build one. But towards the end of 2025, AI (with Claude Opus 4.5) got *so good* that I could have it build an electronics circuit for me (with a lot of cross-checking by other AIs). I sent it out for manufacturing and assembly, and a few weeks later I got a circuit board. I 3D printed an enclosure, purchased the LED lights I wanted from AliExpress, wired everything up and there it was: my perfect light. I had successfully used the strengths of AI—the ability to do what I specified—in combination with my own strengths—my idea and my taste.

But this example wasn’t purely execution or even judgment. I mean, I don’t know what a good printed circuit board for USB-C and WiFi looks like! My engineering degree taught me some basics but I’ve worked with software ever since. I had no idea that you needed so much protection against electrostatic discharge, that it was so important to place capacitors (of multiple capacitances) close to the power input of the microcontroller, or that driving two LEDs on the same board requires some extra components to make sure the electromagnetic signals don’t interfere with each other. Until the whole light was assembled, I could not judge its quality. That was purely ChatGPT and Claude “arguing with each other.”

When you think about it, this makes sense. Claude could remember the entire collected work of circuit board design literature in a split second, together with probably hundreds of thousands of examples of people critiquing others’ circuits. It knows the physics of sunlight radiation much better than the average electronics engineer, and the details of electromagnetic radiation from a switching signal over copper traces much better than the average lighting expert.

So my Claude wasn’t *only* executing the precise things I was asking of it. You see, everything we do is just *too full of decisions* for an AI to be any kind of useful if we have to make all of them. It starts with very small decisions, like, when I ask it to draft an email, whether it opens with “Hi” or “Hello”—please don’t stop to ask me this, I don’t care! But there’s a slippery slope: I also don’t want to have to choose between two options for a specific capacitor where there’s barely any difference between them. Then I’d rather have the AI decide how much electrostatic protection I’d need. And before you know it, the whole project has most of its choices made with AI.

We discussed in Part 1 how important it is to be a good judge when you’re working with AI: it needs to know what *very high quality work* looks like. But as models have been getting better, they need less and less explanation on this. In my own experience with programming, over time I’ve *reduced* the general instructions that tell the model “how to write good software” because it got better at it itself, and at that point adding extra instructions is just a source of distraction and actually reduces the quality of its work. So AI is getting close to, and soon will perhaps be much better at judgment than us humans. In any case, the sheer scale of information it can process puts it in a much better position to give holistic, grounded answers. For example, I just instructed ChatGPT Pro to read through the news from recent years and find studies from the past years and decades describing how civilization is evolving, and asked it what the most important question for humanity is right now. Here’s what it came up with:

> How can humanity govern its rapidly growing power—even under competition—so that it expands broadly shared human agency and flourishing across generations, rather than concentrating control or causing irreversible harm?
> 
> Or, in plainer language: **How do we make power serve life?**

Man, I don’t know about you, but I might actually agree! It’s a very apt observation of civilization: we’re gaining power more quickly than we’re learning how to deal with it, and that’s a dangerous thing. If we took ChatGPT’s judgment on what humanity’s energy should be aimed at, I think we’d be in a very good place.

Abundant execution, abundant attention, abundant judgment. Besides *doing things* and *knowing what to do*, what else is there? Is there anything?

Well, there’s a subtlety in between those two: this judgment call of ChatGPT I agreed with, so if I was someone in power I could take action with that. That’s fine, but what if it gave me something I *didn’t* agree with? Maybe it’s right, maybe it’s wrong. Should I follow its advice, being aware of all that intelligence and research? And if I do and it ends up being wrong, what happens then?

“With AI, we’ll have to worry less about executing the task. As humans, we’ll be the ones responsible for the decisions—we will choose *what* to do, but not have to do it.” It’s a common, rather romantic view on how AI is impacting the way we live. Maybe this wins you an argument with your friends about AI but if you think it through, you quickly realize it’s hanging by a thread. The AI is plenty capable of making the majority of decisions. So the human’s role needs to be something else still. What?

Well, between knowing what to do and doing it lies a decision and more importantly, *responsibility*. Any action that’s taken is a consequence of *someone who decided* that action should be taken—even “fully automated” actions, because someone still laid down the rules beforehand.

### Responsibility

An artificial intelligence is not a legal entity that can bear responsibilities and carry consequences on its own. Even the most advanced agentic AIs or self-running companies are ultimately instructed by a human, who consequently holds the end responsibility for what the AI makes.

Many notaries are very well-paid even though they don’t have a technically very challenging job (I think). They’re valuable because they are ultimately responsible for the consequences if something goes wrong. Signing something is easy, but putting your reputation and license to do your job on the line multiple times per day is something else.

You can’t sue the AI for the contract it drafted in two seconds…but *using* AI, the notary can draft a lot more contracts in the same day. That means a larger portion of their work will be the truly valuable part and so you can expect this person to have a more valuable job because of artificial intelligence. At the same time, I expect that job to become more difficult: notaries will be burdened by having to review document after document with the same scrutinizing intensity to stay trustworthy, while constantly seeing the shiny option of just trusting the AI (or letting another AI review the work). The natural peace they had because writing is an inherently slower process, where it’s easier to focus, is gone.

As the cost of executing keeps dropping, the *share of our work* that comes down to responsibility will keep increasing. Work that has more dramatic consequences if mistakes are made, but is relatively easy to execute, will become massively more valuable. Think notaries as I explained above, but also engineers working on critical infrastructure and even artists: if they can create their art with less work, they have to take responsibility for their *ideas* since those are what make or break the whole artwork.

In the same vein, extremely successful entrepreneurship (I don’t mean “existing business model, just done properly”) is very, very out of distribution. To become a unicorn founder, you need to be one of the only people who figures out a need that a large number of people are willing to pay a ton of money for. By definition, it has to be something that has not been done yet, and that’s quite different from what already exists. But coming up with a new idea isn’t the only thing you need to become a successful startup founder. You need grit, perseverance and a maniacal conviction that this will work even if it takes multiple years of effort and is very different from what there was before. Most people around them will disagree with them, they have to keep running without much proof that they’re on a good path, all the judgment calls they see are against them and they have to choose to pursue their path anyways. You can only do this with full responsibility.

### Is that it?

Responsibility is an odd one on our ladder of *what becomes valuable*: execution, attention and judgment became abundant because AI got better at them, but no amount of “getting better” can allow you to be sued.

In humans, when they have responsibility, they tend to act with more scrutiny and they’ll pay more attention to make sure people don’t get unfairly hurt. AI can’t be held responsible, in fact being responsible *doesn’t make sense* for AI. (Maybe prompt it “make no mistakes or you go to jail.” Just to be sure.)

The capability to bear responsibility is—more than anything—something we’ve *decided* AI cannot do, so of course we still need humans for it. It wouldn’t make too much sense to have the AI carry its own consequences: if you “jail” some AI model because it took some horrible action, it won’t do a “better job” after its punishment. That’s because of the same thing we saw in the creativity chapter: every chat is completely fresh, it has no memory of what happened before.

Does that mean our existence has degraded to being the meatbags that take the punches in place of the machine that did everything, just because a machine can’t feel the pain of the punches? Is that the only thing that will remain valuable?

Maybe.

I know: it feels extremely meaningless.

Is there still *anything* left for us? Other than just…standing in the middle of the tsunami of things that AI creates?

I think we need to ascend more if we want to find an answer. Let’s jump off our ladder—upwards.



## AI as Infinite Creation

AI generates unceasingly, everything, forever. It’s the only thing it does, and it’s stuck in its own loop of creation until something stops it (literally, that’s how it works). If we want to ask what *really* is valuable, durably, we have to go to the extreme. Because if you don’t, you’re just chasing little gaps AI can’t fill today but will be able to tomorrow. So let’s think of AI as this: **Artificial Intelligence is creation itself**.

When you look at it that way, you’ll find some unexpected and surprisingly deep connections.

It does feel in a way like creativity is something “very human.” Is creativity purely about creation? And if not, what’s the *other thing*? Well, the opposite of creation isn’t destruction (that’s just another form of creation) so in a way, creation is *everything*—even our meditative thoughts. The opposite of *everything* is *nothing*—the space around all that is and holds it there, the thing defining the edge of our universe, but also the thing that gives everything its meaning. Infinite *stuff* is worthless by itself; the ocean is shaped by what’s holding it, a painting is defined by the edges of the canvas.

Many religions and spiritual traditions make this distinction.

In **Sāṃkhya**, Hinduism, there’s **Prakriti**, nature, source of mind, matter and all manifestation. She works together with **Purusha**, pure consciousness, the witness, completely inactive—he just *is*.

In **tantric** traditions, **Shakti** is creative power, energy, manifestation and **Shiva** is consciousness, stillness, or the absolute.

**Taoism** has **Wéi**, relentless doing, forceful action; and **Wú Wéi**, which is more like *effortless action*—the Tao does nothing, yet nothing is undone.

**Christianity** has the sisters Martha and Mary; **Martha** representing the active, busy, sometimes chaotic life of giving, doing, serving; and **Mary**, representing the still, contemplative life. Being present, listening.

> “Martha, Martha, you are worried about many things, but few things are needed—indeed only one. Mary has chosen the better part.” – Jesus

The list goes on and on, back all the way from the oldest indigenous cultures to modern continental philosophy.

In continental philosophy, **poiesis** is the process of making, in all forms: producing, recombining, naming, optimizing, even *thinking* itself (no matter how still you sit). The opposite pole is **semiosis**, the *unmade*: the witness, the source, the chooser.

You cannot have one without the other. Pure awareness and stillness is a corpse, infinite making is a tumor. The play between them is what creates our world.

Here’s the thing: poiesis is incredibly attractive. Of course it is, it’s the source of everything! So it’s also where all beauty comes from—this is why in some of these traditions, this side of the coin is represented as a woman. But we’ve also seen it much more tangibly in how we get addicted to scrolling our media feeds, and now in prompting more and more and more to keep the AI making things.

We’re building endless slop. Captivated by unbounded creation. Creating tumors. We can’t help ourselves.

So what will the world, underneath all of this, be secretly craving for more and more?

Stillness. People that can sit, see, and not be pulled away by the current. Those who can *hold* the current instead, those who shape the riverbanks, those who draw the edges and create the meaning. Those who know what *not* to make. Those who strip away everything they do and just *are*—the realest possible form of authenticity. Those who aren’t making, but those who make *matter*. These people add nothing, but somehow add the most important thing of all: a place for the things to be in, something that gives them meaning.

AI can write a song, but it can’t write it with the metaphorical blood of someone who’s been heartbroken. AI can make the pitch deck, but it can’t infuse it with the founder’s fears and determination. AI can generate a painting, but can’t give it a reason for existing.

What remains after infinite creation? Consecration. The human power to set something apart from infinite possibility and bind it to meaning and responsibility.

I think this will be both an opportunity and a challenge for us humans. We can’t get away anymore with “simple” things. When there are a million love songs being created, only the one with that blood of the heartbroken writer will be valued. As long as we keep hiding our pain, our fear, our inner world, we’ll keep making mediocre things that AI can just as easily create too. That’s a scary idea—but if you ask me, *if* we manage to bring humanity to be more open and vulnerable this way, that’ll be an incredibly beautiful consequence of AI.





## When Superintelligence Turns Away


If we imagine a superintelligence exists, it’s not too wild to think that this intelligence could be very aware of what impacts our lives—positive and negative—and over what period of time. This all-knowing intelligence would know when we should take the mountain trail: it could tell you what to do and set up your environment so that you receive exactly the right amount of hardship and challenge so that it makes you optimally happy. It could tell you that today you’ll have to weed the cornfield by hand (between all the machines that are way faster and better than you) because you’ve been having a life that’s too easy for the past few weeks. It would make your partner cheat on you because it knows that you’ll get over it and become a more genuinely happy person afterwards.

Why does this sound so dystopian? Why does that make us recoil when we hear it?

A few years after my brain surgery I had to undergo a second one: there was a small tumor that they left untouched the first time, but it showed up colored on a follow-up scan indicating it might be active, so they had to take it out too. Recovering after the second one was much, much easier compared to the first one. Partly because it was a “smaller” surgery, but for the most part because I *knew what to expect*. The first time, I didn’t know if the pain I was feeling was good or bad, what amount of pain I should be tolerating, when it should be stopping, and so forth. Going through that process of uncertainty, going to the hospital out of fear that I wasn’t healing properly, seeing it was fine—that all contributed to the growth I experienced and contributed to my eudaimonic well-being. After the second surgery, I knew exactly what to expect. The mountain trail was trodden and known down to the tiniest of details. I knew what the hurt would be like, I knew when it would go away, I knew I didn’t have to worry too much about it. The recovery from this surgery was a lot more peaceful; but it didn’t make me go through a personal growth phase in the same way as that first one.

So it looks like a key ingredient for “growth through adversity” is in fact that we *don’t know*. If we know exactly how long the pain will last and how much pain there will be, we don’t have to struggle through it as much and we won’t integrate a great lesson afterwards. In the same way, I think that if you received a text message from the all-knowing AI that your partner will cheat on you today because you need the growth, you’d just blame the AI. You wouldn’t have to go through the same agonizing pain of trying to answer the question of “why did they cheat on me” and get to the conclusion that you’ll never find out and have to live the rest of your life regardless.

Couldn’t we still *choose* our adversity somehow? Many of us (like me) do genuinely enjoy the struggle of pushing hard weights around in the gym for the satisfaction of having a fit, functional and beautiful body. It’s real hard work and it produces real meaning. But honestly it’s not the same level, is it? There’s a very different texture to the meaning you derive from having survived a near-death experience you didn’t ask for and surviving a dangerous bungee jump where you knew all the risks beforehand.

This gets us to the odd conclusion that probably, an artificial superintelligence that’s truly benevolent towards humans will just *leave us*. It might leave a mini-version of itself to help us get some basic necessities for everyone, a narrow superintelligence here and there for things like curing cancer, but apart from that, the general all-knowing superintelligence will realize that it shouldn’t give us everything we want—for our own sake—and it can’t deliberately make things difficult for us either because *we’ll know it’s from the AI* and it won’t mean as much anymore.

But maybe, just maybe, this superintelligence doesn’t *actually leave*. Maybe they hide, and they do their benevolent activity either way. Maybe they give us good things but also bad things in life, in exactly the proportion and exactly at the times we need them to maximally grow our happiness. It’s just that we can’t know if they did it deliberately or not, because if we knew for sure, the meaning would be lost. The doubt about whether it’s there or not is part of the game.

This is the point in my reasoning where I had to suddenly stop. Looking at that last paragraph, it seems like I’m describing…God?

You’re telling me that the ideal machine we would build for maximizing human life satisfaction would look exactly like every god in every religion on the planet? How did our engineering approach to build the machine that optimizes human happiness end up creating God? It makes sense, in a way: religion has *always* been one of humanity’s best ways for giving meaning to their lives. Our ancestors survived because they found peace and meaning in assuming that their hardship was caused by a benevolent god that needed them to suffer somehow. That belief made them through those rough times they all encountered. But our ancestors also literally faced *real* hardship—their lives were at stake. The fact that all the way on the other side of the spectrum, where everything can be taken care of for us, we seem to find the *exact same thing* is…bewildering. That whole setup is an incredible type of “happiness technology” and in a way it doesn’t even matter whether anything is actually there! Religion worked to give people meaning before any technology (though you can argue religion itself being a form of technology-as-an-idea), and somehow it ends up being the ultimate technology when we have everything, too. The belief that *maybe* there is a benevolent god-like figure is what we need, whether that’s there or not.




# Conclusion



Humanity has thrived on this planet because we’ve always had a knack for noticing *tension*: the difference between how the world is and how it could be. Where we could make things easier for us in the future, where we could improve things, where we can get more comfortable.

These rapid advances in AI are now getting us to the point where we’re forced to ask the question: *Wait, what if we actually succeed? What if we build something that can solve all problems and make everything as comfortable as we always wanted it to be?* Because what we’re starting to see is that we’ve reached the point where more ease isn’t bringing us more life satisfaction. When there’s no more disease to solve, when our food gets delivered to our doorstep in a few minutes, when infinite entertainment is at our fingertips, when we have the perfect friends and lovers right there digitally…when everything we want is easy to get, the feeling starts to gnaw at us that somehow, *it all doesn’t seem to mean anything anymore.*

We used to have to learn for decades to become a master painter, now some graphics card in a data center spits out a masterpiece the moment you describe it. We used to make embarrassing typing mistakes in the emails to our boss, now each message is perfectly polished. We used to be scared to admit our most shameful acts to our closest friends, now we know for sure our virtual companion will accept us and give us love.

Why does it all feel so empty?

Here’s a clue: we have chosen to make a machine (from where, by the way? who or what in us made that choice?) that works, at its base, by predicting the next most-likely thing—the word, the image, the sound that has the highest probability of emerging next. The minimal error, the thing that is least likely to be wrong. You could call this The Inevitable: generative AI completes what *had* to come out.

But our whole human neurochemical system works to make us pay attention to the *unlikely*, to *surprise*, to things that are *different* from what we expected. Dopamine encodes reward prediction error: things ending up better than expected. The reason many AI creations feel so dull is because they’re a dull starting point scaled up, stamped out, repeated ad nauseam. When something fascinating emerges from AI, it’s most definitely because some human gave it a unique, distinct starting point.

There’s a fundamental mismatch between the machine making The Inevitable and our mind yearning for The Surprise. They’re not opposites—you can only define surprise in contrast to some baseline—but AI just can’t make something “special” because it lacks a proper ability to create novel things. Even when we inject random noise (which always happens), somehow we humans “smell” it’s not real. The shape of AI’s noise somehow isn’t right and we notice it’s slop.

Why? Well, first, today’s AI models are frozen. Once a language model (or image, video, audio, anything) is done training, it doesn’t evolve anymore. It can adapt to new memes if you teach it, but it can’t feel what variations of a meme have been repeated to death. It’s an old photo perpetually trying to pretend it’s a live video stream, so it can’t *feel* what’s truly new, what’s old—it’s fundamentally unable to smell slop like we can.

Second, for AI, there are no consequences. Once it has stopped generating (or more precisely, when someone forces it to stop), the model is deactivated and disappears from the picture. There is no lasting impact, no consequences for being boring or being interesting. It can’t *care* about its randomness because it doesn’t matter.

Things that have real meaning to us come from what you might call *risky randomness*: intentionally doing something unexpected, that might or might not work out. Something that’s perfectly predictable is meaningless, but something completely random is too.

So AI isn’t taking away meaning. It’s shoving meaning into our face. It’s pushing us away from what lacked meaning anyways. AI is the embodiment of The Inevitable. The Surprise can only exist *in contrast to* what’s inevitable, so this technology is like a big fat arrow pointing at what actually, truly, deeply matters.

Before, having worked on something for a long time was what gave something meaning. But if nothing takes time to make anymore, what is the source of meaning? What’s the deeper layer that AI doesn’t have?

We have *something* and I think it’s exactly in the place we’re trying to avoid. The thing we’re trying to fix: our chaos.

You can’t make a computer do something without a goal. The goal has to come from somewhere and it can’t just be another computer—that would just mean you’re kicking the can down the road. And once you give it the goal, it will optimize and figure out how to get there. Any imperfection is just a bump in the road. Any randomness is just a tool to march towards the optimum.

The thing that gives us the surprise, the meaning, is when something decides to *not* go to that optimum. To leave the imperfection as it is. Not as an intermediate state—as a line-in-the-sand decision that has real consequences. The notary making the call, the artist releasing the love song, the painter leaving her painting alone.

We grow through struggle because it throws us in an unexpected direction, and planned struggle doesn’t work the same way. My second brain tumor surgery was “just another thing.” We can only consecrate through *decision*—it wouldn’t carry the same weight if you just picked the top of the leaderboard automatically. The love we give feels special when it’s given *despite* clear reasons why not to. Just imagine the difference between someone who became your partner because they rank number one in ‘compatibility with you’ of all people, versus you knowingly deciding to love them even though you clearly see all of their flaws.

In fact, when you think about it, the only reason anyone does anything is *because things aren’t perfect*. Every move of anything is sourced in the tension—the struggle—of the unresolved: the problem that’s pending, the stomach that wants to be fuller, the heart that yearns for more love. We wouldn’t do anything in a world of perfection.

Struggle *is* the force of life.

So when you look at these perfect machines, doing their perfect thing without getting tired, without stopping, without complaining—understand that they’re missing the one thing that could make them matter: accepting imperfection.

It’s the most profound of our realizations that we find at the end of the tunnel of our darkest periods. It’s the exact thing we can never recreate without true chaotic struggle. It’s what gives us peace: after days or weeks or months of sitting in the darkness and trying to answer *why is this happening to me,* we decide to let it go. We lift our eyes again, we give up on trying to find the answer, we realize we don’t need to be perfectly optimized to live a happy life, and we go on with our lives.

I’m sitting here, somehow typing with my laptop on the sideboard instead of a table or my desk. There’s a bunch of opened boxes around me, laundry that’s been waiting to be folded for a week, I’m angry at myself for procrastinating and mucking around when I told myself I was going to write the book. My hands have this weird thing like every summer where the skin flakes. I’m a bit frustrated at how much dust is on all the things sitting around the laptop. But I still decided to sit down and write. I feel the tension of all these things to be solved, the struggle of not being happy with my behavior, I feel the tick of the clock eating every second into that empty void of the past—to never return again. I’m ashamed of myself, bad things happened to me without any reason and oftentimes I just sit there, doing literally nothing, staring outside, feeling my life—all of life—slowly disappear.

But that’s what makes this moment so special. We all know we’re marching towards our end, we all know the sun will die at some point so everything we’ll ever make will disappear. It’s like our last few minutes…and what do we do? We dance. We write books. We just sit there gossiping about meaningless things with our friends while time ticks away. We decide to offer these precious moments to love and to creation. Just for the sake of having a beautiful dance. It’s impossible to spend our lives perfectly—everything disappears at some point—so the fact that we decide to be here anyways and live fully is, I think, the most beautiful thing there is.

So, when AI does all the work for us, will we be happier?

Turns out the happiness we thought of—pleasure—isn’t what we need. What we need is something that makes it all make sense, something that gives everything *meaning*. And we find it right in the place AI can never reach: our weird idiosyncrasies, our decisions that come from a place we can’t describe, the questions and doubts and imperfections that we just let exist…the fact that we still decide to dance.
